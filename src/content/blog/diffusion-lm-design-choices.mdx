## Introduction

The design space for DLMs is combinatorially larger than autoregressive, as it encompasses a wide range of different generative trajectories. All autoregressive models follow the same left-to-right generative path. Diffusion language models have come to mean any generative method that leverages some form of parallel, iterative refinement in the generative process. This encompasses Gaussian diffusion; Riemannian Flow Matching on the sphere; Diffusion on the Simplex; Discrete Corruption via uniform random token corruption; diffusion in a latent space with an autoregressive decoder; masked token corruption, or both. For recent overviews, see Li et al. [@li2025dlmsurvey] for diffusion language models broadly, and Tang et al. [@tang2026survey] for continuous diffusion and flow language models. 

This rapid expansion in diffusion approaches have resulted in many common techniques being introduced under one diffusion family, but gradually becoming used in many others. As an example, we look at self-conditioning, or feeding the model's previous predictions as part of the next input. This technique was first introduced within a [@chen2022analog] within a binary Gaussian diffusion method. However, this technique has been shown to be effective Gaussian diffusion on embeddings [@strudel2022self] [@dieleman2022continuous], spherical flow matching [@chemseddine2026spherical], and simplex diffusion [@deschenaux2026simplex]. Block diffusion was introduced for masked diffusion, but the general approach has been adopted into uniform diffusion [@diffusiongemma2026], and gaussian diffusion [@yang2026sigma], where it blends naturally with continuous noise schedules that increase positionally left to right [@chavan2026lafa; @schiff2026clock]. 

The fields shift to more flexible recipes has led to both the need to more thoroughly understand when and where certain techniques improve performance, along with the convenience of being able to quickly incorporate techniques across recipes from different papers. Teasing apart why techniques transfer requires being able to quickly evaluate them within new recipes through careful ablations. Furthermore, designing effective recipes requires understanding when and where different techniques are needed. Returning to the example of self conditioning -- in [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], we hypthesized that self-conditioning corrects an imbalance induced by long-tailed pathalogies of continuous corruption behavior in high dimensions. But if this is the case, then why does it benefit pure discrete diffusion in DiffusionGemma [@diffusiongemma2026]? And what other recipes will self conditioning be beneficial towards? 

To answer questions like this, we introduce **SDDC**: **S**ome **D**LM **D**esign **C**hoices, a codebase designed to make DLM recipe's as modular as possible. We focus our attention on a specific archetype of DLM recipes: we address recipes where 1) noise is applied to factorized representations, as opposed to contextual; 2) the recipe is generally tokenizer agnostic; and 3) training is simulation free. We explain DLM recipes as answering three core questions: 
1. ***Information Path***: How is information represented, and how is it destroyed during the generative path? 
2. ***Denoising Task***: How do we learn to traverse this information path? 
3. ***Inference Settings***: How do we actually use both the learned model and information path to generate samples? 

Outside of these questions, we define recipe modifiers as components that require changes across all three facets. In this blog, we briefly the core design philosophy of this design approach, and why we believe this is the right approach for studying DLMs. 

## Inspiration: Hackable Diffusion

The structure of SDDC borrows heavily from [Hackable Diffusion](https://github.com/google/hackable_diffusion) [@hackablediffusion2026], a JAX toolbox for experimenting with and teaching diffusion models. Hackable Diffusion is built on two principles: assemble models from small objects with well-defined contracts, and give each part of the pipeline (architecture, corruption, inference, loss and sampling) its own sub-library. Its core file, `hd_api.py`, defines nothing but the interfaces between these parts, so a training or sampling loop never needs to know which schedule, network or data type sits behind them.

SDDC applies the same split to diffusion language models. Most of its components have a direct counterpart in Hackable Diffusion:


<HackableComparison />

SDDC departs from Hackable Diffusion in three places, each driven by its focus on language:

- **Same data, several views.** Hackable Diffusion's `Nested` pattern applies different processes to different parts of the data, such as an image and its label. A hybrid view in SDDC applies several processes to the *same* tokens. The views share one clean sequence and one clean-token prediction, and their noisy states never need to map into each other.
- **Representation as its own part.** In Hackable Diffusion, mapping tokens into vectors happens inside the network. SDDC pulls it out into a representation with three operations (`encode`, `posterior_mean`, `decode`). That boundary is what lets one-hot and embedding Gaussian diffusion share one algorithm and one set of samplers, and it is where most continuous language-model recipes actually differ.
- **Recipes as configurations.** Hackable Diffusion composes Python objects directly. SDDC assembles the same kind of pieces from Hydra configs, ships published methods as named recipes, and checks at build time that the chosen parts fit together. The goal is to run many controlled comparisons, where changing one part should be a one-line override.

## Scope: What is this Codebase?

The codebase captures a narrow slice of the DLM landscape. Every method in it has three properties:

1. It works with **factorized** representations.
2. It does not alter the **tokenization** process.
3. Its training loop is **simulation free**, except for the extra pass needed for self conditioning.

Below we explain what each constraint means and which works it excludes. They are excluded for no reason other than time. Each excluded direction is important for DLM research, and leaving it out is a current flaw of the codebase.

### Factorized Representations

Every method here corrupts a representation with a one-to-one mapping to the tokens of the original sequence. Position $i$ has its own state, and its clean value depends on token $i$ alone: a one-hot vector or a point on the simplex, a learned embedding of that token, or a point on a sphere. Discrete methods use the token itself. Given a token, you know its clean representation; given the clean representation, you can read the token back. The forward process then corrupts each position from its own clean value, and the noisy state can be a mask, a random token, or a noisy point in that space. The model still sees the whole sequence when it denoises. Only the representation and the noise are per token.

This rules out contextual representations, even ones that keep one vector per token. The hidden state of a BERT model or a T5 encoder at position $i$ depends on every token in the input. The same token gets a different vector in every sentence, and the vector at position $i$ carries information about its neighbors. Corrupting that vector corrupts a mixture of the whole sequence, not one token, and no fixed rule reads token $i$ back from it. The same goes for compressed, sequence-level latent spaces, where one latent vector describes many tokens at once [@lovelace2023latent; @meshchaninov2026cosmoscompressedsmoothlatent; @shen2026codar]. Both change what "one step of denoising" means, and comparing them fairly against per-token methods needs its own protocol.

### Tokenizer Agnostic

The tokenizer is fixed before diffusion starts. Every recipe sees the same token sequence, and the diffusion process never merges, splits, or learns tokens. This lets us swap tokenizers as an experimental variable (we do this below, from raw bytes up to a 10k-token BPE vocabulary) without changing anything else in the recipe.

It rules out methods that learn their own segmentation or work on dynamic patches. It also rules out methods that split each token into smaller units and diffuse those. CoBit [@batzolis2026towards] is one example: it writes each token's ID as a fixed-width binary code and runs continuous diffusion over the resulting bitstream, so the model predicts about $\log_2 V$ bits per token instead of one choice among $V$ tokens. The insertion and deletion modifier changes sequence length, but it inserts and deletes whole tokens from the same fixed vocabulary.

### Simulation Free

Training never runs the model's own sampler. Each training example is built in closed form: sample a time, corrupt the clean sequence with the forward process, and ask the model to undo it. The one exception is self conditioning, which runs one extra forward pass to produce the feedback it trains on.

This rules out distillation, consistency, and flow map methods that train on the model's own trajectories [@roos2026categoricalflowmaps; @lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous], as well as RL-style fine-tuning. These methods are central to fast sampling. They are also expensive to train, and their results depend on the base model they start from, which makes them hard to compare at this scale.

## The design space

Each part of a recipe answers one question and is made of a few pieces. Modifiers sit across the parts, because each one touches more than one of them.

| Part | Question | Pieces, with examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Information path | What does the model observe at each noise level? | **Representation** (tokens, one-hot vectors, learned embeddings, points on the simplex or the sphere), **corruption process** (masking, uniform replacement, Gaussian, Dirichlet, spherical, vMF), **noise schedule** (fixed, rank-calibrated, or learned like CDCD) |
| Denoising task | What must the model predict, and how is it trained? | **Network** (one transformer with swappable input, time-conditioning and output slots), **parameterization** (clean-token posterior, plug-in estimate, score), **objective** (cross-entropy, ELBO-weighted losses, score entropy), **training time sampler** (uniform or learned) |
| Inference | How do predictions become a generated sequence? | **Initialization**, the **time grid**, the **update rule** (the method's own reverse step, renoising, entropy-bounded commitment, ODE solvers) and **terminal decoding** |
| Modifiers | Cut across the parts | **Self conditioning**, **block diffusion** (including encoder-decoder splits), **hybrid views** that corrupt the same tokens several ways, **insertion and deletion** |

{/* Representation */}

<MapText name="about.rep">

Within our framework, representation means the space the noise lives in. In the codebase, we focus on a fairly narrow selection of representations: tokens, one-hot vectors, and embeddings. We choose these as they can be thought of as "factorized" representations: within the representation being noised, there is a one-to-one correspendence between the original tokens in the sequence and the noised elements in the sequence. This is in contrast to contextual representations, where the representation are a function of the entire sequence.



- SDDC has two representations: `one_hot`, where each token is a vector of length $V$, and `embedding`, a lookup into a table $E$.
- Both expose the same three operations: `encode` turns tokens into states, `posterior_mean` turns predicted token probabilities into an expected clean state ($pE$), and `decode` turns a state back into tokens. Because of this shared interface, one-hot and embedding Gaussian diffusion run through the same training code and the same samplers.
- Some noise schedules only make sense for one geometry: the rank-calibrated schedules need one-hot vectors, and the sphere-rank schedule needs unit-norm embeddings. These requirements are checked when a recipe is built.
- Contextual or compressed encoders would need more than a new `encode` and `decode`: prompts and the posterior mean would have to be redefined, which is why they sit outside the current scope.
- Reconstructing text well and being easy to generate are different properties of a representation [@zhang2026diffusibility; @du2026decoder].

</MapText>

<MapText name="rep.tokens">

Here, the corruption is defined to occur on tokens directly, rather than some continuous vector. This does not mean that the process is free of continuous vectors: every token is eventually mapped to a continuous vector in the embeding table. Does working with tokens allow for a more "native" corruption process for text? Or is it just disguising the fact that the network must operate on continuous vectors? This is still an open debate. 



- Used by every discrete process in the codebase: masking and both forms of uniform noise. The network still looks each token up in its own embedding table.
- Recipes on token IDs include MDLM, MD4, RADD, D3PM, SEDD, LLaDA-style and ARDM-style masking, UDLM, DUO, and discrete flow matching.
- The usual argument for continuous states: sampling many tokens in parallel from per-position marginals can mix modes when only a few steps are taken, while a continuous state is transformed jointly [@tang2026survey; @lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous].

</MapText>

<MapText name="rep.onehot">

Each token is mapped to a vector of dimension $|V|$ where very coordinate is zero, except for the coordinate correspending to the original token index. Such representations are convenient, as they are learning free and still provide a meaningful map from discrete to the continuous domain. However, these representations are generally expensive to work with, especially with modern LLM vocabularies.  



- The posterior mean of a one-hot vector is just the vector of token probabilities, and decoding is an argmax. Nothing is learned.
- Used by the one-hot Gaussian recipes (FLM-style and the rank-calibrated baselines), by CANDI's continuous view, and by simplex diffusion.
- The rank and argmax-error schedules are computed exactly from one-hot geometry, so they are only available here.
- The state has size $L \times V$, so the input projection grows with the vocabulary. This matters when we sweep the tokenizer.
- FLM pairs this representation with cross-entropy and a time reparameterization [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous].

</MapText>

<MapText name="rep.embed">

Each word is a learned point in a vector space (two dimensions shown). Highlighted points are the five tokens. As we are focused on factorized representations within the context of this blog series, these embedding vectors are obtained from the initial embedding table used by the model as opposed to some encoder network. 



- The table is trainable by default and can be frozen. Rows can be kept at unit norm, optionally renormalized after every optimizer step.
- Decoding takes the argmax of $zE^\top$, which is nearest-neighbor decoding when the rows have unit norm.
- Used by the CDCD-, LangFlow- and RePlaid-style recipes, embedding flow matching, and the spherical recipes.
- The average of unit-norm embeddings is shorter than one, and the codebase keeps it that way: its length still says how uncertain the prediction is.
- Embeddings learned jointly with the denoiser can collapse together or grow until the noise stops mattering. Published fixes include cross-entropy with normalized embeddings [@dieleman2022continuous; @chen2026langflow] and a full likelihood bound [@gulrajani2023likelihood; @yang2026continuousdiffusionscalescompetitively].

</MapText>

{/* Corruption */}

<MapText name="about.corr">

How we decide to remove information from the clean sequence, and consequently create information during inference. This is one of the more consequential decisons in terms of the overal DLM recipe: many continuous methods share some form of self-conditioning and an adaptive noise schedule akin to [@dieleman2022continuous]. If we chose to work with discrete corruption, some components of the network -- such as time conditioning -- would no longer be necessary. 


- The codebase has six corruption processes: absorbing (masking), uniform, uniform with a posterior reverse step, Gaussian, spherical, and Dirichlet (simplex).
- A process only owns the math of corrupting and un-corrupting. It never calls the network; the sampler decides when each step happens.
- A hybrid is not a new process. Any set of views (masked, uniform, Gaussian, simplex, spherical) can run side by side, each with its own schedule and clock, and their embeddings feed one transformer. The views share the clean tokens and nothing else.
- Discrete and continuous corruption are closer than they look: uniform discrete diffusion is what you get by taking the argmax of Gaussian diffusion on one-hot vectors [@sahooDiffusionDuality2025], and sticky jump diffusions describe discrete, continuous and hybrid processes in one framework [@jutrasdube2026sticky].

</MapText>

<MapText name="corr.masked">

Masked: each token is independently hidden behind [M]. A hidden token carries no information about its word. The process itself is an absorbing state diffusion process: once a token is corrupted in the forward process, it no longer changes identity. Similarly, once a token leaves the absorbing process and realizes some token, it does not change. However, it is possible to define remasking processes that allow for self-correction. 



- Each token survives with probability $\alpha_t$ and otherwise becomes [M]. Stepping from $t$ to a cleaner time $s$, a masked token is revealed with probability $(\alpha_s-\alpha_t)/(1-\alpha_t)$ and drawn from the predicted clean posterior.
- The process handles revealing; samplers such as entropy-bounded decoding only choose which positions to reveal.
- Remasking is available at inference only, through the forward-resampling and churn samplers.
- The masked ELBO reduces to weighted cross-entropy [@sahooSimpleEffectiveMasked2024; @shiSimplifiedGeneralizedMasked2024], and the network does not need to know the time [@ou2025absorbingdiscretediffusionsecretly; @zheng2024masked].

</MapText>

<MapText name="corr.uniform">

Uniform: each token is independently replaced by a random word (red). Ideally, this would represent a more difficult task as it is no longer clear which words are "clean" and which words are "noisy". However, in practice it is often clear, as the distribution of uniformly random tokens is quite different from the actual marginal distribution over tokens. 



- Both uniform processes share the forward step, $q(x_t=j \mid x_0=i)=\alpha_t\,1[i=j]+(1-\alpha_t)/V$, and differ in the reverse step.
- The plug-in version (UDLM, DUO) works with a normalized estimate that is not a true posterior. The posterior version mixes exact reverse bridges over a predicted clean-token posterior. Each needs its matching output, so they are not interchangeable.
- DUO adds a curriculum that starts from a softened relaxation of the tokens [@sahooDiffusionDuality2025]; UDLM is the plain plug-in recipe [@schiff2024simple].

</MapText>

<MapText name="corr.gaussian-onehot">

Gaussian on one-hot vectors: noise is added to every entry. As t grows the true word’s bar sinks into the noise. As discussed in [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous], this results in poor scaling of discrete identity corruption, which makes the learning problem challenging. 



- This uses the same Gaussian process as embeddings, $z_t=\alpha_t e(x_0)+\sigma_t\epsilon$. Only the representation changes.
- Two schedules exist only for one-hot vectors: one calibrates noise by the expected fraction of wrong coordinates that beat the right one, $\Phi(-1/(\sqrt{2}\sigma))$; the other by the chance that argmax picks the wrong token.
- Recipes: FLM-style (an error-based clock), the rank-calibrated one-hot baselines, and the continuous view of CANDI.
- Token identity becomes recoverable inside a narrow window of noise levels, and the window shrinks as the vocabulary grows [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion; @lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous].

</MapText>

<MapText name="corr.gaussian-embed">

Gaussian on embeddings: noise pushes each point off its word. The blob shows how far noise can reach at this t. Embedding diffusion can be difficult to train stably, as using an MSE objective would result in the embeddings collapsing to the same vector; whereas using a cross-entropy objective results in 



- Schedules available here: exponential VE, linear interpolation, a sphere-rank calibration, LangFlow's Gumbel schedule, rescaled versions of these, loss-fitted clocks, and RePlaid's learned log-SNR.
- Recipes: CDCD-style, LangFlow-style, RePlaid, embedding flow matching, and embedding VE.
- The network predicts a clean-token posterior; the sampler turns it into an expected embedding and from there into a velocity.
- Earlier work handled embedding collapse in different ways: a regression loss plus a norm penalty [@li2022diffusion], cross-entropy with normalized embeddings [@dieleman2022continuous], or an anchor loss that forces predictions to decode correctly [@gao2022empowering].

</MapText>

<MapText name="corr.simplex">

Simplex: each token is a probability vector. Noise drifts it from its corner toward the uniform middle while it always sums to one.



- The state is a point on the simplex, corrupted with Dirichlet noise and reversed with Dirichlet bridges. The network can read it as an expected embedding or through its argmax.
- The recipe follows Simplex Diffusion Models [@deschenaux2026simplex]. Its sampler supports churn and guidance, and its time grid can be linear, cosine, or fitted to the training loss.
- Related simplex methods use other paths: Dirichlet flow matching [@stark2024dirichlet], or Gaussian noise on logits as in SSD-LM and TESS [@hanSSDLMSemiautoregressiveSimplexbased2022; @mahabadi2024tess].

</MapText>

<MapText name="corr.spherical">

Spherical: embeddings live on the unit sphere. Noise picks a random point and moves along the arc toward it, so the state never leaves the sphere.



- Embeddings live on the unit sphere, and corruption moves along it using the sphere's log and exp maps.
- Two recipes: S-FLM's geodesic path [@deschenaux2026language], and von Mises-Fisher paths with an optional Langevin corrector at inference [@chemseddine2026spherical].
- Sampling averages the tangent directions toward each candidate token, weighted by the predicted probabilities.
- Other spherical approaches: Fisher-Flow maps the simplex onto the sphere with a square root [@davis2024fisher], RDLM builds bridges toward token vertices [@joContinuousDiffusionModel2025], and spherical Brownian diffusion uses heat-kernel noise [@miele2026sbd].

</MapText>

<MapText name="corr.combined">

Two corruptions run on the same clean sequence, each in its own representation and with its own schedule. The network sees both views at once. (CANDI is masked + Gaussian.)



- Each view bundles a process, a schedule, a representation and a clock. Their embeddings are summed by default; gated fusion can turn a view on or off depending on whether a token is masked.
- CANDI: masking plus Gaussian noise on one-hot vectors, with mask-dependent fusion and a rank-calibrated Gaussian schedule [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion].
- CADD-style: the continuous view is only used while a token is still masked [@zheng2025continuouslyaugmenteddiscretediffusion].
- The RePlaid hybrid: masking plus Gaussian noise trained with a likelihood bound, with revealed tokens fed back into the continuous input [@yang2026continuousdiffusionscalescompetitively].
- Other pairs (spherical plus uniform, simplex plus Gaussian) compose the same way, though we have not tuned them.
- Related: joint token and latent denoising in CCDD [@zhou2025coevolutionary].

</MapText>

{/* Noise schedule */}

<MapText name="about.sched">

How much noise there is at each time t. Three families: fixed before training, a few learned knobs, or fully refit from the loss.



- A schedule only maps time to coefficients: a keep rate $\alpha_t$ for discrete views, or $\alpha_t$ and $\sigma_t$ with their derivatives for Gaussian ones. It never chooses training times or corrupts anything.
- The options range from fixed curves (log-linear, rank-calibrated, exponential VE, linear), through LangFlow's Gumbel schedule, to clocks refit from the loss and RePlaid's learned schedule.
- Rescaling a Gaussian path (VE, VP or linear) keeps its signal-to-noise ratio, but not the exact network inputs or the path a finite-step sampler takes.
- For a fixed Gaussian path, changing the schedule or the training-time distribution amounts to reweighting the loss across noise levels [@kingma2021variational; @dieleman2022continuous].

</MapText>

<MapText name="sched.fixed">

Predefined schedules never change: the same curve is used from the first training step to the last.



- Discrete views default to log-linear. For Gaussian noise, the rank and argmax-error schedules set noise by how recoverable the clean token is, and exponential VE and linear interpolation are the plain choices.

</MapText>

<MapText name="sched.semi">

Semi-adaptive: the schedule comes from a fixed family (here a Gumbel-shaped one, as in LangFlow), and only its few parameters are fit to the training loss. The fit pushes the loss to grow linearly with t (right), but a two-knob family can only get so straight. Press Play to watch the fit.



- The Gumbel schedule can fit its location, scale and an entropy term to the per-example cross-entropy. These knobs are updated by their own small optimizer step, separate from the network's, and start from LangFlow's released values [@chen2026langflow].

</MapText>

<MapText name="sched.full">

Fully adaptive: the whole curve is refit from the training loss, as in CDCD. The map from t to noise level is warped (left) until the loss grows linearly with t (right), so equal steps in t cover equal amounts of denoising difficulty. RePlaid also learns its schedule, but to lower the variance of its likelihood estimate rather than to straighten the loss. Press Play to watch the fit. Loss-derivative fitting reaches the same warp another way: it spends time in proportion to how fast the loss changes, which also makes the loss rise linearly in t. Because it reshapes how the process moves through t, in training and in generation, it is a noise schedule, not a training time sampler.



- The CDCD- and S-FLM-style clocks keep a buffer of recent losses and refit the time warp every 50 steps, after 1,000 warmup steps, with an exponential moving average.
- The CDCD-style clock fits the warp with isotonic regression over 100 bins; the CDCD paper learns its bins by gradient [@dieleman2022continuous]. The S-FLM-style clock uses a spline fit.
- RePlaid's schedule works differently: a monotone curve trained by gradient to lower the variance of the likelihood estimate [@yang2026continuousdiffusionscalescompetitively].

</MapText>

<MapText name="sched.full-discrete">

For masked or uniform noise it warps the keep rate.

</MapText>

<MapText name="sched.combined">

With two corruptions, each view has its own schedule.



- Each view keeps its own schedule and its own map from global time. The RePlaid hybrid instead shares one learned clock across its views.

</MapText>

{/* Network */}

<MapText name="about.net">

A transformer reads the noisy sequence and produces predictions.



- Every diffusion recipe uses the same network: a DiT [@peebles2023scalable] whose input, time conditioning, self-conditioning and output head are swappable slots. Autoregressive baselines use a causal version with a KV cache.
- The default size is width 512, 8 blocks and 8 heads.
- The time input can be raw time or a transform of it, such as $-\log(1-t)$, the log keep rate, or log SNR. This changes only what the network sees, not the noise.
- The transformer blocks can also be changed: other sublayers in place of the MLP, and other ways for a block to read earlier blocks.
- Other designs: ELF feeds time to the network as extra tokens instead of through adaptive normalization [@hu2026elf], and LangDiT revisits these choices for continuous text models [@luo2026langdit].

</MapText>

<MapText name="net.self-off">

Self-conditioning off: each pass starts fresh from the noisy input.

</MapText>

<MapText name="net.self-mean">

Self-conditioning on the expected embedding: the previous pass’s probabilities p are multiplied by the embedding table E, giving the average embedding ē the model expects. That vector is fed back in.



- The network gets back the expected clean embedding from its previous prediction, before any temperature or top-k, through a projection that starts at zero.
- In training, 90% of examples first get a no-gradient pass on the same noisy input, and that prediction is fed back. In generation the previous step's prediction is reused, so no extra network calls are needed.
- A second option re-runs the two passes fresh at every step, at twice the cost.
- It works with every method that predicts a clean-token posterior. Plug-in and score methods (UDLM, DUO, SEDD) cannot use it, and neither can the adaptive ODE solver.
- Self-conditioning was introduced in Analog Bits [@chen2022analog]. Proposed explanations include fixed-point refinement [@yooSelfconditionedFlowMap2026a] and making tokens identifiable under heavy noise [@davis2026design]. It can also push models toward repetition [@zhang2026ace].
- Self-conditioning sits here as a network input, but it also changes the effective information path. [Part 3](/blog/diffusion-lm-design-choices-sc-interpretation/) gives our working interpretation.

</MapText>

<MapText name="net.self-hidden">

Self-conditioning on the hidden state: the previous pass’s last hidden vector h is fed back directly, before it is turned into word probabilities.



- The final hidden state is fed back through a layer norm whose scale and bias start at zero, with no projection. Unlike the expected embedding, this does not need token probabilities.
- Related: Simplex Diffusion Models feed back hidden features [@deschenaux2026simplex], and loopholing does the same for discrete diffusion [@joLoopholingDiscreteDiffusion2026].

</MapText>

<MapText name="net.time-off">

Without time conditioning the network must infer the noise level from the input itself.



- One switch removes time from the input embedding, every block and the output head together. The RADD recipe runs without it.
- For masked diffusion this is principled: the optimal clean-token posterior does not depend on time [@ou2025absorbingdiscretediffusionsecretly; @zheng2024masked].

</MapText>

<MapText name="net.time-on">

Time conditioning tells the network how noisy its input is.

</MapText>

{/* Output */}

<MapText name="about.param">

What the network’s output means.



- The codebase separates what the network outputs from what that output means. The main meanings are a clean-token posterior (with or without the masking rules), a plug-in estimate for uniform noise, score ratios for SEDD, and a posterior with a Gaussian prior term for RePlaid.
- Two normalized outputs are not interchangeable just because both sum to one: each is tied to the reverse step and loss that expect it.
- Continuous recipes predict a clean-token posterior, which the sampler converts to an expected clean state and then to a velocity.
- Logits are shaped in a fixed order: an optional softcap, then excluded tokens, then (only when sampling) temperature, top-k and top-p, then normalization. Entropy-bounded sampling measures entropy before any token is committed.
- For Gaussian paths, clean-data, noise, velocity and score predictions can all be converted into each other; FLM and ELF both find clean-data prediction the easiest to learn [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous; @hu2026elf].

</MapText>

<MapText name="param.posterior">

For every position, a probability over the six words for the clean token. Slide the input noise up and the bars flatten.



- For masking, the posterior never predicts [M] and copies visible tokens through. The Gaussian version skips both rules, since a noisy vector is not an observed token.
- CDCD uses the same kind of output: it averages embeddings under the posterior and turns that into a score [@dieleman2022continuous].

</MapText>

<MapText name="param.score">

Score ratios: how much more likely each word is than the current token. Not a normalized distribution. SEDD defines them for uniform noise too; the codebase implements the masked version.



- The output is a set of log score ratios, not a distribution. It is used by the SEDD recipe, with score entropy and its analytic sampler [@louDiscreteDiffusionModeling2023].

</MapText>

<MapText name="param.plugin">

A plug-in estimate for uniform noise: the posterior mixed with a uniform floor that grows with noise.



- Only uniform-noise recipes (UDLM, DUO) use this output. Hybrid views and expected-embedding self-conditioning need a true posterior, so they reject it.

</MapText>

<MapText name="param.gpost">

A clean-token posterior shaped by a Gaussian prior on the noisy vector, used to compute the expected clean vector.



- Adds $\frac{\alpha}{\sigma^2}\, z E^\top$ to the logits before normalizing, so each word gets a bonus for lining up with the current noisy vector. Used by the RePlaid recipes [@yang2026continuousdiffusionscalescompetitively].

</MapText>

<MapText name="param.next">

Only the next position is predicted, given everything to its left.

</MapText>

{/* Loss */}

<MapText name="about.loss">

There are two ways to arrive at an objective. A **likelihood-based** objective starts from the probabilistic model the other choices define and trains on a bound on its log-likelihood, so its form follows from those choices. An **endpoint-based** objective starts from inference instead: it trains the network on exactly the quantity the sampler will ask for, the clean tokens at the end of the path.

In practice the two routes are closer than they look. The term compared at each noise level (cross-entropy on tokens, MSE on embeddings, or score entropy) is fixed by the representation and the parameterization, not by which route you take. What mostly differs is how much each noise level counts, and whether the final loss value is a bound you can report. With unlimited capacity, both are minimized by the same clean-token posterior. Gaussian noise is the main exception: its likelihood bound compares embeddings with MSE, while the endpoint loss compares tokens with cross-entropy. Finally, weighting a noise level more heavily and sampling it more often have the same effect, which ties the choice of objective to the training-time sampler below.



- Available objectives: MDLM's weighted cross-entropy, plain cross-entropy with optional weighting, the uniform-noise loss of UDLM and DUO, score entropy, the D3PM bound, RePlaid's Gaussian ELBO, a hybrid ELBO, and losses composed from KL terms.
- Plain cross-entropy can be weighted by time, $\alpha$, $\sigma$, effective noise, SNR or log SNR, or by a learned weight that starts out as plain cross-entropy.
- The Gaussian ELBO reports its prior, reconstruction and diffusion terms separately, so the learned schedule and the time sampler can adapt to each one.
- Block diffusion can add a next-token loss on the clean context on top of the base objective.
- Cross-entropy is a strictly proper scoring rule, so it is minimized by the true token probabilities [@gneiting2007strictly]. A full likelihood bound also penalizes embedding collapse on its own [@gulrajani2023likelihood].

</MapText>

{/* Equations shown above the loss picture. Colours: termint (amber, the integral), termmse (purple), termrecon (teal), termprior (rose), termoff (grey, a term that is zero). */}

<MapText name="eq.likelihood-masked">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_{i:\, z_{t,i} = [\mathrm{M}]} \textcolor{termint}{\frac{-\kappa'(t)}{1-\kappa(t)}}\,\big(-\log p_{\theta,i}(x_i)\big) \;+\; \textcolor{termoff}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}} \;+\; \textcolor{termoff}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}}
$$

With $\kappa(t) = 1 - t$ the weight is $1/t$. Both end terms are zero: every token is visible at $t = 0$, and at $t = 1$ every token is [M], which is the prior.

</MapText>

<MapText name="eq.likelihood-uniform">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_{i} \textcolor{termint}{\frac{\kappa'(t)}{V\kappa(t)}}\Big[\frac{V}{\bar x_{z}} - \frac{V}{\bar x_{\theta,z}} - \sum_{v} \frac{\bar x_v}{\bar x_z}\log\frac{\bar x_{\theta,z}\,\bar x_v}{\bar x_{\theta,v}\,\bar x_z}\Big] \;+\; \textcolor{termoff}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}} \;+\; \textcolor{termoff}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}}
$$

Here $z = z_{t,i}$ is the token now at position $i$, $\bar x = V\kappa(t)\,\mathbb{1}_{x_i} + (1-\kappa(t))\,\mathbf{1}$ and $\bar x_\theta = V\kappa(t)\,p_{\theta,i} + (1-\kappa(t))\,\mathbf{1}$: the true and predicted marginals, times $V$. Every position counts, since any token may have been swapped. Both end terms are zero: at $t = 1$ the tokens are already uniform, which is the prior.

</MapText>

<MapText name="eq.likelihood-gaussian">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \textcolor{termrecon}{\sum_i -\log p_{\theta,i}(x_i \mid z_0)} \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_i \textcolor{termint}{w(t)}\,\lVert e_i - \hat e_{\theta,i} \rVert^2 \;+\; \textcolor{termprior}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}}
$$

With $z_{t,i} = \alpha_t e_i + \sigma_t \epsilon_i$, $w(t) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{SNR}'(t)$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}} = \sum_i \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\alpha_1 e_i, \sigma_1^2 I)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0, I)\big)$. All three terms are present: $\gamma$ is finite at both ends, so neither end is exact.

</MapText>

<MapText name="eq.likelihood-hybrid">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \textcolor{termoff}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}} \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_{i:\, K_{t,i} = 0} \Big[\textcolor{termint}{\frac{-\kappa'(t)}{1-\kappa(t)}}\big(-\log p_{\theta,i}(x_i)\big) + \textcolor{termmse}{w(t)}\,\lVert e_i - \hat e_{\theta,i} \rVert^2\Big] \;+\; \textcolor{termprior}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}}
$$

The cross-entropy scores tokens as they are revealed; the MSE scores hidden tokens that stay hidden. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} = 0$ because every token is visible at $t = 0$; the prior KL stays because $\gamma$ is finite at $t = 1$.

</MapText>

<MapText name="eq.likelihood-score">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_{i:\, z_{t,i} = [\mathrm{M}]} \textcolor{termint}{\sigma'(t)}\Big[\sum_{v \neq [\mathrm{M}]} s_{\theta,i}(v) - r_t \log s_{\theta,i}(x_i) + r_t(\log r_t - 1)\Big] \;+\; \textcolor{termoff}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}} \;+\; \textcolor{termoff}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{prior}}}
$$

With $\sigma(t) = -\log\kappa(t)$ and $r_t = \kappa(t)/(1-\kappa(t))$, the true ratio for the hidden token; $s_{\theta,i}(v)$ are the network's score ratios. The last term is a constant that makes the minimum zero.

</MapText>

<MapText name="eq.endpoint-masked">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_{i:\, z_{t,i} = [\mathrm{M}]} \textcolor{termint}{1}\cdot\big(-\log p_{\theta,i}(x_i)\big)
$$

The same term as the likelihood loss, with weight 1 at every noise level. Not a bound.

</MapText>

<MapText name="eq.endpoint-uniform">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_{i} \textcolor{termint}{1}\cdot\big(-\log p_{\theta,i}(x_i)\big)
$$

Every position counts, since any token may have been swapped. Not a bound.

</MapText>

<MapText name="eq.endpoint-continuous">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_{i} \textcolor{termint}{1}\cdot\big(-\log p_{\theta,i}(x_i \mid z_t)\big)
$$

The network reads the noisy vectors $z_t$ and predicts the clean token directly, as in CDCD: no distance in vector space and no end terms. Simplex and spherical noise have no bound in the codebase, so this is their only objective.

</MapText>

<MapText name="eq.endpoint-hybrid">

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}_q \sum_{i} \textcolor{termint}{1}\cdot\big(-\log p_{\theta,i}(x_i \mid z_t)\big)
$$

One cross-entropy, with the network reading both views of each position. Not a bound.

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.endpoint">

Endpoint-based: predict the clean tokens with cross-entropy, at every noise level alike, because the clean-token prediction is what the sampler uses. It is not a bound on any likelihood. For masked noise, the term at each noise level is the same as in the likelihood-based loss; only the weighting across noise levels differs.

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.endpoint-discrete">

With masked or uniform noise this is plain cross-entropy on the clean tokens.

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.endpoint-continuous">

With continuous noise it is the CDCD-style recipe: cross-entropy on the clean token, predicted from the noisy vectors, rather than a distance in vector space.

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.endpoint-hybrid">

With two views, one cross-entropy covers both: the network predicts the clean token from both views at once.

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.likelihood">

Likelihood-based: the loss is whatever a bound on the log-likelihood (an ELBO) turns out to be for the other choices. That bound depends on the parameterization as well as the corruption: DUO’s bound is written in terms of its plug-in posterior, and SEDD’s in terms of score ratios.

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.likelihood-masked">

For masking it is cross-entropy weighted by 1/t on the masked positions (as in MDLM), so nearly clean examples count much more.

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.likelihood-uniform">

For uniform noise it is DUO’s exact bound, which compares the model’s plug-in posterior with the true posterior over every word, not only the correct one.

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.likelihood-gaussian">

For Gaussian noise it is a weighted MSE between the expected embedding ē and the true embedding, integrated over time, plus two end terms: a reconstruction cross-entropy at t = 0 and a prior KL at t = 1 (RePlaid’s three terms).

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.likelihood-hybrid">

For masked + Gaussian it is both at once: cross-entropy on the masked positions, weighted by how fast the keep rate falls, plus the Gaussian weighted MSE on the embeddings and the prior KL (the REPLAiD hybrid).

</MapText>

<MapText name="loss.likelihood-score">

For score-ratio outputs it is score entropy (SEDD).

</MapText>

{/* Training time sampler */}

<MapText name="about.tsamp">

Which times t each training batch uses. It is worth explaining why the training time sampler is distinct from the noise schedule, as these are often grouped together. We treat these separately as they serve distinct purposes: the noise schedule defines the physical noise level at each time. In other words, the noise schedule defines what a time value $t$ means towards the actual denoising task difficulty. The training time sampler decides how to allocate budget across different denoising tasks -- for example, we may want to investigate what happens if we allocate more training towards the middle of the noise schedule. If we link the noising process and training time sampler, then this would require changing what noise level a value of $t$ correspends to. Decoupling the two allows us to keep the same interpretation of $t$ while altering the allocation of training budget. A principal goal of this codebase is to maximize the flexibility for DLM recipes. As decoupling training time sampler from the noise schedule increases the number of design choices, we opt towards separating the two. 

- Options: stratified uniform times (the default, spread evenly across GPUs and gradient-accumulation steps), D3PM's discrete steps, RePlaid's split between reconstruction and diffusion examples, and a learned importance density for the RePlaid hybrid.
- Changing which times are trained does not change the inference grid, and it does not reweight the loss unless the recipe does so explicitly.
- When insertion is on, the loss-derivative warp (a noise schedule) can also fit to the insertion losses.
- Stratified, low-discrepancy time sampling comes from VDM [@kingma2021variational].

</MapText>

<MapText name="tsamp.uniform">

Uniform times between ε and 1. The codebase stratifies them: a batch of n examples gets one time in each of n equal slices, so every batch covers the whole range. This is the default for almost every recipe.

</MapText>

<MapText name="tsamp.discrete">

D3PM trains on a grid of discrete steps (1000 in the codebase, 10 shown). Each time is a stratified uniform draw, rounded up to the next step.

</MapText>

<MapText name="tsamp.mid">

Fixed non-uniform: training visits the middle half of t three times as often as either end (the `cdcd_mid_range_times` preset). Each draw is divided by its density, so the objective is unchanged; only where the gradient estimate is most precise moves. The noise schedule, and so generation, is untouched.

</MapText>

<MapText name="tsamp.alloc">

Adaptive allocation (RePlaid) splits each batch between the two sampled terms of the Gaussian likelihood loss. The reconstruction term lives at a single point, t = 0, so it cannot be reached by drawing t from a range: some rows sit exactly at t = 0 to estimate it (red), and the rest get stratified uniform times for the diffusion integral. The prior term at t = 1 is computed exactly and needs no samples.

The split follows how noisy each term is: each gets a share of the batch proportional to the running standard deviation of its loss (Neyman allocation), so the noisier term gets more rows and the total estimate has the lowest variance. Each term is rescaled by how many rows it received, so the objective stays the same bound whatever the split. Like every training time sampler, it changes only how training spends its batch, not what t means or how the model generates. It needs the three-term Gaussian likelihood loss, which is why it is only available for Gaussian noise with the likelihood-based objective.

</MapText>

<MapText name="tsamp.mix">

With a block-size mixture, single-token blocks skip this and always train at t = 1 (pure noise).

</MapText>

<MapText name="tsamp.note">

The sampler never changes what a value of t means; that is the noise schedule’s job. It only decides how often each t is drawn (left), and so how the training budget is split across t (right). Generation never uses it.

</MapText>

{/* Sampler */}

<MapText name="about.samp">

How predictions become text. Four settings: where to start, which steps to take, how each step changes the state, and how the end state becomes tokens.



- Fixed-length recipes share one sampling engine with independent settings: how to propose clean tokens (keep the full posterior, sample, or argmax), which positions to update (all, random, or by entropy), how to move the state (the method's reverse step, or fresh forward noise), whether committed tokens stay fixed, and how to decode at the end.
- The named samplers are presets of this engine: ancestral, entropy-bounded, SEDD's analytic step, discrete flow matching, Euler, Heun and adaptive ODE solvers, DDPM, and each process's native reverse step.
- Cost is counted in network calls, including the final decoding call, so samplers should be compared at equal call budgets.
- There are two kinds of temperature: one on the token probabilities, and Plaid's score temperature, which applies only to Gaussian steps.
- In RePlaid's comparison, DPM-Solver++ does best with few steps and DDPM with many [@yang2026continuousdiffusionscalescompetitively; @lu2025dpmsolverpp]. The codebase currently has Euler, Heun, an adaptive-step ODE solver and DDPM.

All of these are settings of one loop; step through it in the figure below the map.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.ancestral">

Ancestral sampling reveals tokens a few at a time in random order, one network call per step.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.eb">

Entropy-bounded sampling fixes a position as soon as the model is sure about it, and fixes every such position in the same step. Positions become final in order of confidence, not at random, so it needs fewer steps and rarely fixes a wrong word.



- Commitment belongs to the sampler, so even a purely continuous model can use entropy-bounded decoding: a committed token's state is set to its clean value and held there.
- A churn option randomly reopens committed tokens and re-noises them with the process's own forward step. It costs no extra calls, but it may use more of the step budget before finishing.
- Based on the entropy-bounded sampler [@benhamu2025accelerated].

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.ode">

A Gaussian solver (Euler, Heun, adaptive ODE or DDPM) moves every vector a little each step; all words sharpen together.



- Euler takes one network call per step, Heun two, and the adaptive-step ODE solver picks its own steps within an error tolerance and a call budget. Integration stops just short of the clean endpoint, then decodes. The adaptive solver does not support self-conditioning.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.flow">

Each process takes its own reverse step, and all positions sharpen together. Simplex Diffusion Models use Dirichlet bridge sampling, with optional churn. S-FLM integrates along the geodesic with tangent Euler steps. vMF spherical flows use an ODE predictor, with optional Langevin corrector steps. On the simplex the state is itself a probability vector, drawn as the bar under each position: it starts even and its mass moves to one word. On the sphere the state is a direction: the dial shows the angle between it (white) and the clean embedding (purple), which closes as generation runs.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.hybrid">

Each view takes its own reverse step: masked tokens are revealed while the continuous view sharpens.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.analytic">

SEDD's analytic step uses the score ratios to jump several masked tokens at once with exact transition probabilities.



- Used only with SEDD's score ratios [@louDiscr eteDiffusionModeling2023].

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.ctmc">

Discrete flow matching follows a continuous-time Markov chain: tokens jump between states, and with uniform noise a filled-in word can still change later (red).



- At each step, sample a candidate token, then jump to it with probability $1-e^{-h\,\mathrm{rate}}$. This is the basic solver from discrete flow matching, with no corrector [@gat2024discrete]. Revealed masked tokens stay fixed; under uniform noise a token can still change.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.ddpm">

DDPM takes stochastic Gaussian reverse steps: every vector sharpens, with a little fresh noise added each step.



- DDPM takes stochastic steps from the reverse Gaussian posterior; it is not Euler with noise added. It has its own self-conditioning options and supports score temperature.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.ar">

Autoregressive decoding: one new token per step, strictly left to right.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.eb-continuous">

With continuous noise the same rule applies to vectors: once a position’s prediction is under the entropy bound, its vector is set to the clean embedding of the predicted word and frozen. The other positions keep taking the method’s own reverse step.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.renoise">

Renoising predicts the whole clean sequence at every step, then corrupts that prediction again to the next, lower noise level. Nothing is final until the end: with masking, a word shown at one step can be masked again at the next (orange).



- Forward resampling draws a full clean sequence from the posterior, then applies fresh forward noise at the next time, separately for each view. It works with any fixed-length recipe, and it can remask tokens.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.renoise-continuous">

With continuous noise, each step jumps to a fresh noisy version of the predicted words instead of moving the current vector a little. Early on the prediction can switch between words; the noise shrinks step by step until one word is left.

</MapText>

<MapText name="samp.note">

Each card is one position. With continuous noise the likely words are stacked, larger and brighter when more likely, and they merge into one as the noise falls. A dashed box is not final; a solid box is fixed. Red marks a wrong word.

</MapText>

{/* Sequence modifiers */}

<MapText name="about.mods">

Each one changes several parts at once. Block diffusion changes the input layout, the attention mask and the order of generation. E2D2 keeps that layout but gives the clean and noisy copies separate weights. Insertion changes which positions exist, adds a length loss and lets the sampler insert tokens.



- Available modifiers: block diffusion (default block size 32, with an optional next-token loss on the clean context and a cache for the clean prefix), the E2D2 encoder-decoder split, BlockGen's mix of block sizes, insertion, self-conditioning, and hybrid views.
- Block diffusion wraps an existing recipe and leaves its process, schedule, output and loss unchanged. RePlaid is not supported yet.
- Insertion treats deletion as the forward corruption and insertion as the reverse step. The number of new tokens in each gap is drawn from a Poisson distribution, and each view initializes new positions its own way: as [M], as uniform noise, or as a noised guess of the clean token. It is an approximate model, not FlexMDM's exact objective.
- Insertion also works inside block diffusion.
- Related work: BD3-LM [@arriolaBlockDiffusionInterpolating2025], E2D2 [@arriola2025e2d2], FlexMDM [@kim2026any], and expanding flows for continuous insertion [@tang2026expanding].

**Hybrid views, and how they differ from earlier hybrids**

- Earlier hybrids fix the pairing as part of the method.
  - CANDI pairs masking with Gaussian noise on one-hot vectors [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion].
  - CADD pairs masking with a Gaussian diffusion in the learned token-embedding space. The continuous state is noised only while its token is masked, and equals the clean embedding once the token is revealed. The network predicts token logits only [@zheng2025continuouslyaugmenteddiscretediffusion].
  - CCDD pairs a discrete diffusion on tokens (masking by default) with a Gaussian diffusion on continuous representations from a fixed encoder, contextual embeddings in its main experiments. The two corruptions are independent given the clean data, and one network has two heads, one per part [@zhou2025coevolutionary].
- Here a hybrid is a modifier, not a method. "Discrete" and "continuous" are just two views of the same clean tokens, and the modifier does not care which corruption each view uses.
- So arbitrary processes can be combined: masked with Gaussian, uniform with simplex, simplex with Gaussian, or more than two views. The one requirement is that every view works from a clean-token prediction.
- The views share three things: the clean tokens, one transformer (their embeddings are fused at the input), and one prediction. Their noisy states never have to map into each other.

**How each view's time is managed**

- There is one global time $t \in [0, 1]$. The training time sampler draws it, and the sampler steps it on one grid.
- CADD and CCDD also run both parts on one shared time, each part with its own schedule. We keep that, and add a time map per view.
- Each view has its own time map, which turns global time into that view's local time. Each view also has its own schedule, which turns local time into a noise level.
- So at the same global time, two views can sit at different noise levels. One view can be nearly clean while the other is still mostly noise.
- A time map can be the identity (the default), a fixed power or linear map, or a learned clock fit to the training loss. A learned clock can be applied to some views and not others.
- At generation, every view moves along the same global grid. Each takes its own reverse step, at its own local time, from the one shared prediction.

</MapText>

<MapText name="mods.none">

Turn on block diffusion or insertion to see how they change generation.

</MapText>

<MapText name="mods.block">

Block diffusion splits the sequence into blocks. In training the network reads the noisy sequence and the clean sequence together (length 2L) under the mask above. At generation, blocks are produced left to right; inside a block, tokens are denoised in parallel.

</MapText>

<MapText name="mods.block-bc">

Block-causal clean context, as in the BD3-LM paper (`block_diffusion.x0_causal=false`).

</MapText>

<MapText name="mods.block-causal">

Token-causal clean context: the clean stream is an ordinary causal language model, which lets every block size share one KV cache (the codebase default, `x0_causal=true`).

</MapText>

<MapText name="mods.block-ar">

Token-causal clean context plus a next-token loss on it, so the same network is also trained as an autoregressive model of the context (`block_diffusion.clean_loss=autoregressive`). BlockGen gets its AR behavior a different way: see Mixture of block sizes.

</MapText>

<MapText name="mods.block-mix">

Mixture of block sizes, as in BlockGen: one model with a token-causal clean context is trained on block sizes 1 and 16. Size 1 uses pure noise and CE, so it is next-token prediction; the larger size keeps the recipe’s own loss (`+experiment=blockgen_mixture`, composed after a fixed-block recipe).

</MapText>

<MapText name="mods.split-e2d2">

Encoder + decoder, as in E2D2: the same block-causal layout, but the clean copy and the noisy copy go through separate weights. An encoder reads the clean blocks once per block; a decoder denoises the current block at every step, and each decoder layer attends to the final encoder state. Encoder and decoder depth are set separately (`encoder_decoder.encoder_layers`; decoder depth is `model.n_blocks`).

</MapText>

<MapText name="mods.insert">

Insertion lets the length change: generation starts short and new tokens are inserted between existing ones (outlined), so the model decides the length.

</MapText>

## Why modular? (And some Teasers)
The modular approach of this codebase is motvated by two core reasons: first, a modular set up makes it easier to design experiments that tease out otherwise subtle interactions that different components can have on downstream performance. For pure continuous methods (Gaussian diffusion, the hyperspherical flows from [@deschenaux2026language], the spherical vMF trajectories from [@chemseddine2026spherical], and simplex diffusion from [@deschenaux2026simplex]), we notice that these methods can run samplers that commit tokens during generation, such as a hybrid formulation of the EB sampler from [@benhamu2025accelerated] or random anchoring. This is surprising, given that none of these methods are trained to handle committed tokens. What is more surprising is that how well this works is contingent on whether self-conditioning is used: without self conditioning, committing samplers are erratic and often perform quite poorly. With self conditioning, they land much closer to the best samplers that never commit a token. This seems to support two conclusions: 
1. Self conditioning makes discrete commitment look more in-distribution for continuous methods. 
2. This is a change in how generation unfolds rather than a guaranteed gain: even with self conditioning, committing tokens rarely beats the best sampler that does not commit. 

This is a fairly naunced finding, and it wouldn't have been discovered without being able to plug and play different components across recipes, especially for inference settings. 

Second, it makes it quite easy to produce new recipes from existing components. Most of the work is re-using the existing components and wiring everything together through a config file, instead of designing each recipe from scratch.

## Future directions

This project will keep changing as we find new questions to ask. The codebase also covers a narrow slice of current DLM research (see [the scope](#scope-what-is-this-codebase)). These are the directions where we would most welcome contributions.

### Sampler distillation

Distillation, consistency and flow map methods [@roos2026categoricalflowmaps; @lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous] are among the most active areas of DLM research, and the codebase leaves them out because they train on the model's own trajectories. SDDC treats many recipes as settings of one skeleton. A shared framework of the same kind for distillation methods would make them far easier to compare.

### Insertion and deletion

The codebase can insert and delete tokens, including a FlexMDM-style recipe [@kim2026any], but the implementation is basic. Much remains to explore: how these modifiers interact with the rest of a recipe, and how to make them work well rather than merely work.

### Latent diffusion and other representations

Latent diffusion for language has made real progress [@lovelace2023latent; @meshchaninov2026cosmoscompressedsmoothlatent; @shen2026codar]. Bringing compressed and contextual latent spaces into the same framework, along with representations that split tokens into smaller units, such as CoBit's bitstreams [@batzolis2026towards], would show which of the results here depend on per-token representations.

These are only a few directions, and we are sure we are missing others.
