## Introduction

Diffusion language models (DLMs) now cover almost any method that turns noise into text, and every recipe makes many design choices. Each published method makes them all at once, often changing several in the same paper. Some of its techniques carry over to other recipes and some do not. Even when one carries over, it is unclear whether it does the same job there.

Image diffusion went through the same phase. EDM [@karras2022elucidating] split diffusion methods into separate parts (the noise schedule, the network's preconditioning, the training noise distribution and the sampler), showed that several published methods were particular settings of those parts, and then studied each part on its own. SDDC (Some DLM Design Choices) tries to do the same for diffusion language models. Its [codebase](https://github.com/patrickpynadath1/sddc) splits every recipe into three parts: the **information path** (how tokens are represented and corrupted, and on what schedule), the **denoising task** (what the network predicts and how it is trained), and **inference** (how the trained model generates). Each part is made of pieces, such as the representation, the corruption process or the sampler, that can be swapped across recipes.

The core claim fits in one sentence: treat the parts as swappable so you can measure how they interact.

The clearest example in this post is self conditioning and the sampler. On text8, self conditioning looks like it hurts masked and uniform diffusion. Hold every model to the same sampler and the picture flips: the models without self conditioning had been winning with a renoising sampler that seems to break on masked diffusion with self conditioning. A comparison that changes one method at a time, each with its own default sampler, would report the first result and miss the interaction.

The experiments are small because our compute is small: 8-layer models, 20,000 training steps and, mostly, one training seed. They are worked examples of the questions a modular setup makes cheap to ask, not conclusive findings, and each ends with a note on how sure we are. The protocols and the analysis behind every number are in the repo, so anyone with more compute can rerun them at scale.

## Why modular?

DLM papers rarely change one thing. A new method often arrives with a new corruption process, a new objective, a new schedule and a new sampler, compared against baselines that differ in all four. When it wins, it is hard to tell which change mattered, or whether the same change would help another recipe. Splitting recipes into parts turns that into a direct question. Keep everything fixed, swap one part, and measure.

Swapping parts does not mean every part works with every other. Some are tied together by theory. A sampler built on a clean-token posterior cannot drive a score parameterization, and a rank-calibrated Gaussian schedule assumes a particular representation. SDDC checks these ties when a recipe is built. The repo's [compatibility table](https://github.com/patrickpynadath1/sddc/blob/main/docs/validation/compatibility.md) is generated by building and running every method family with every sampler and modifier, so each cell either works or states why the combination is refused.

Many DLM papers build on shared codebases, usually within one family of methods. SDDC differs in that it spans discrete and continuous methods (masked, uniform, Gaussian, simplex, spherical, vMF and their hybrids) under one interface, with one training and evaluation pipeline.

## Scope: What is this Codebase?

The codebase does not capture every design choice in the DLM landscape. It captures a narrow slice. Every method in it has three properties:

1. It works with **factorized** representations.
2. It does not alter the **tokenization** process.
3. Its training loop is **simulation free**, except for the extra pass needed for self conditioning.

Below we explain what each constraint means and which works it excludes. They are excluded for no reason other than time. Each excluded direction is important for DLM research, and leaving it out is a current flaw of the codebase.

### Factorized Representations

Every method here corrupts a representation with a one-to-one mapping to the tokens of the original sequence. Position $i$ has its own state, and its clean value depends on token $i$ alone: a one-hot vector or a point on the simplex, a learned embedding of that token, or a point on a sphere. Discrete methods use the token itself. Given a token, you know its clean representation; given the clean representation, you can read the token back. The forward process then corrupts each position from its own clean value, and the noisy state can be a mask, a random token, or a noisy point in that space. The model still sees the whole sequence when it denoises. Only the representation and the noise are per token.

This rules out contextual representations, even ones that keep one vector per token. The hidden state of a BERT model or a T5 encoder at position $i$ depends on every token in the input. The same token gets a different vector in every sentence, and the vector at position $i$ carries information about its neighbors. Corrupting that vector corrupts a mixture of the whole sequence, not one token, and there is no fixed way to read token $i$ back from it. The same goes for compressed, sequence-level latent spaces, where one latent vector describes many tokens at once [@lovelace2023latent; @meshchaninov2026cosmoscompressedsmoothlatent; @shen2026codar]. Both change what "one step of denoising" means, and comparing them fairly against per-token methods needs its own protocol.

### Tokenizer Agnostic

The tokenizer is fixed before diffusion starts. Every recipe sees the same token sequence, and the diffusion process never merges, splits, or learns tokens. This lets us swap tokenizers as an experimental variable (we do this below, from raw bytes up to a 10k-token BPE vocabulary) without changing anything else in the recipe.

It rules out methods that learn their own segmentation or work on dynamic patches. It also rules out methods that split each token into smaller units and diffuse those. CoBit [@batzolis2026towards] is one example: it writes each token's ID as a fixed-width binary code and runs continuous diffusion over the resulting bitstream, so the model predicts about $\log_2 V$ bits per token instead of one choice among $V$ tokens. The insertion and deletion modifier changes sequence length, but it inserts and deletes whole tokens from the same fixed vocabulary.

### Simulation Free

Training never runs the model's own sampler. Each training example is built in closed form: sample a time, corrupt the clean sequence with the forward process, and ask the model to undo it. The one exception is self conditioning, which runs one extra forward pass to produce the feedback it trains on.

This rules out distillation, consistency, and flow map methods that train on the model's own trajectories [@roos2026categoricalflowmaps; @lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous], as well as RL-style fine-tuning. These methods matter a lot for fast sampling. They are also expensive to train, and their results depend on the base model they start from, which makes them hard to compare at this scale.

### What this Codebase and Blog Is Not

It is not a leaderboard. The tasks and model sizes are chosen so that a single question can be answered with a few dozen small runs, not so that any method looks its best. It is not large-scale pretraining. And it is not a faithful reproduction of every paper it borrows from: where our configs differ from a paper, the docs say so.

## The design space

Each part of a recipe answers one question and is made of a few pieces. Modifiers sit across the parts, because each one touches more than one of them.

| Part | Question | Pieces, with examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Information path | What does the model observe at each noise level? | **Representation** (tokens, one-hot vectors, learned embeddings, points on the simplex or the sphere), **corruption process** (masking, uniform replacement, Gaussian, Dirichlet, spherical, vMF, blurring), **noise schedule** (fixed, rank-calibrated, or learned like CDCD) |
| Denoising task | What must the model predict, and how is it trained? | **Network** (one transformer with swappable input, time-conditioning and output slots), **parameterization** (clean-token posterior, plug-in estimate, score), **objective** (cross-entropy, ELBO-weighted losses, score entropy), **training time sampler** (uniform or learned) |
| Inference | How do predictions become a generated sequence? | **Initialization**, the **time grid**, the **update rule** (the method's own reverse step, renoising, entropy-bounded commitment, ODE solvers) and **terminal decoding** |
| Modifiers | Cut across the parts | **Self conditioning**, **block diffusion** (including encoder-decoder splits), **hybrid views** that corrupt the same tokens several ways, **insertion and deletion** |

## The Experimental Pipeline

In total, this codebase supports about 3,000 recipes, not counting samplers, modifiers, and architectural changes. A grid search over all of them is simply impractical. Instead, the codebase is built for targeted experiments that vary a few design choices at a time.

<Pipeline client:visible />

The pipeline figure shows how we go from a question to an answer. First, given a question, we define a protocol: the variations of a recipe we want to compare, and the tasks we want to compare them on. Each variation is trained once, which gives us one checkpoint. Each checkpoint is then tuned over inference settings. We pick the sampler with the best validation score, and only that winner is scored on held-out test examples with fresh seeds.

A large part of this approach is inspired by [@rowell2025modelfactory], specifically the way design choices are quickly translated into reliable downstream performance numbers. Given the enormous difference in scale and goal (training models versus studying diffusion methods), this project ended up being substantially different.

### Training Pipeline and Supported Tasks

All models in this post share one backbone: a diffusion transformer with 8 layers, width 512, and 8 heads. Unless noted, every model trains for 20,000 updates at batch size 256, with learning rate 3e-4 and 2,500 warmup steps. Where a paper gives a loss weighting or a training time distribution, we use the paper's choice.

We use three tasks, each with a metric computed on generated samples rather than on the training loss:

- **Sudoku (hard split):** fill in a puzzle given its clues. Metric: the share of puzzles solved exactly.
- **text8:** unconditional character-level generation on Wikipedia text. Metric: unique valid words per sample.
- **TinyStories:** short children's stories, with either raw bytes or a BPE vocabulary of 500 to 10,000 tokens. Metric: corpus BLEU against held-out reference stories.

The codebase also includes OpenWebText (generative perplexity) and TinyGSM (math word problems), but we do not use them here.

<div className="callout">
<span className="callout-label">Why no likelihoods?</span>

Every number in this post is computed on generated samples, never on a likelihood or a bound on it. Most recipes here train on a variational bound (an ELBO), but each corruption process gives a different bound with its own unknown gap to the true likelihood, so comparing bounds across, say, masked and Gaussian diffusion compares their slack as much as their models. Several recipes do not train on a bound at all, so their validation loss is only a training signal. And even an exact likelihood says little about samples at this scale, where generation is the end goal: a model can have a good likelihood and poor samples, or the reverse [@theis2016noteevaluationgenerativemodels]. Sample-based metrics have their own trap, since a single sampler setting can trade diversity for quality and decide a comparison on its own, which is why every model here is scored with its own tuned sampler. We make the full argument in [Generative Frontiers](/blog/eval_methodology/) [@pynadath2026generativefrontiers].

</div>

### A note on BLEU

Corpus BLEU has two parts. The first counts how many word sequences in the model's continuations also appear in the real endings. The second is a brevity penalty: if the continuations are shorter overall than the real endings, the score is multiplied by a factor below one.

On TinyStories, that penalty matters more than we expected. Every story is generated in a fixed-length canvas, and the model decides where to end it. Masked diffusion models end their stories early, at 76 to 92% of the real length, and the penalty takes 8 to 27% off their score. Most other runs write stories close to the real length and lose little; the exceptions are simplex without SC, the block diffusion models, and a few uniform runs. So a ranking by BLEU is partly a ranking by story length.

Whether ending early should count against a model is a judgment call. So every TinyStories BLEU in this post can be shown both ways, and the switch in the figure below sets all of them at once. Where the choice changes a conclusion, the text says so. For the tokenizer ladder, we also asked a judge model to read the stories; it sides with the penalty-free ranking (see "A second opinion from a judge model" below).

<BleuLength client:visible />

<ExpandableSection title="Why we do not tune training settings" client:load>

We keep training settings at paper defaults across methods. This is not ideal: we apply these methods to tasks the original papers did not cover, and the defaults could misrepresent an otherwise good method.

The reason is cost. For diffusion models, the settings that matter most are not only the learning rate. The loss weighting and the distribution over training times also matter a lot. But changing the loss weighting changes the objective itself, so two models trained with different weightings have validation losses that measure different things. They cannot be ranked by validation loss.

The only fair way to compare training settings is downstream: tune the sampler for each candidate, then compare sample quality. That turns every training run into a full round of inference tuning. A single question in this post already takes 10 to 50 training runs. Tuning training settings on top of that would multiply the cost several times over.

So our claims are scoped to this protocol: paper-default training, a shared budget, and inference tuned per model on validation data. Most work at this scale also uses default training settings; the inference tuning is the part we add.

</ExpandableSection>

### Inference Pipeline

Unless noted, every diffusion model gets 128 network evaluations (NFE) per sequence. Every sampler starts from the model's prediction of the clean tokens at each step; they differ in how they use it:

- **Native** takes one step of the method's own reverse process, using the model's softmax at a tuned temperature as the clean estimate. For Gaussian diffusion, that means the expected embedding under the softmax, followed by a draw from the Gaussian reverse step. For masked diffusion, each masked position unmasks with the schedule's probability and gets a token sampled from the softmax.
- **Native greedy** is the same step with the argmax token as the clean estimate.
- **EB greedy** commits argmax tokens wherever the model's entropy is below a tuned bound, following entropy-bounded unmasking for masked diffusion [@benhamu2025accelerated].
- **Renoise** draws a full clean sequence (sampled or argmax), then corrupts it again with the forward process down to the next noise level.

Each sampler's own settings, such as the temperature or the entropy bound, are tuned with a small automatic search on validation data. As the next sections show, the best sampler often depends on the rest of the recipe, so tuning it per model is not optional.

## Self Conditioning: What does it do?

Self conditioning has emerged as a necessary part of continuous diffusion language models (cDLMs). First introduced in [@Chen_Zhang_Hinton_2023], it has proven effective across a wide range of cDLM recipes [@dieleman2022continuous; @strudel2022self; @batzolis2026towards; @yang2026continuousdiffusionscalescompetitively]. Some works have tried to remove it because of its added cost at training and inference [@davis2026design]. Others have embraced it and leaned further in [@deschenauxLoopholingAllWay2026a; @yooSelfconditionedFlowMap2026a]. Either way, there is almost no debate about whether self conditioning works. The open question is why.

Several works offer reasonable answers:

1. In [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], we hypothesized that self conditioning anchors the model, because discrete and continuous corruption are imbalanced.
2. In [@yooSelfconditionedFlowMap2026a], the authors show that self-conditioned flows are equivalent to a fixed point iteration.
3. In [@davis2026design; @shabalinWhyGaussianDiffusion2026], the authors argue that self conditioning fixes a mismatch between training and inference.

These answers are fairly convincing. But it is unclear whether they capture the full picture. [@joLoopholingDiscreteDiffusion2026] shows that loopholing also improves discrete diffusion with uniform and masked corruption, and [@deschenauxLoopholingAllWay2026a] shows that self conditioning alone is a fairly strong generative method. None of the three hypotheses explains these results, since each depends on properties of continuous diffusion. If self conditioning helps discrete diffusion, where those reasons do not apply, do the theories fully explain why it works in the continuous case? We test three narrower questions below. Two more, about tokenizers and about a judge model's view of the stories, are among the further examples after them.

In all of these experiments, "SC" means the model receives the expected embedding under its previous prediction.

### Is self conditioning just a deeper network?

#### The question

Self conditioning runs the network twice during training, so a fair question is whether it only adds depth. If it does, a 16-layer model without SC should match an 8-layer model with SC. If SC does something extra depth cannot, the 8-layer SC model should win.

#### The experiment

For each corruption type we need three models: an 8-layer baseline, the same baseline with SC, and a 16-layer model without SC. The baseline puts both changes on one scale: how much does each one add over the same starting point? We run this for Gaussian corruption (with the CDCD learned clock), masked corruption (ELBO weighting), and uniform corruption, on all three tasks. That is 27 models. TinyStories uses the 10k-token BPE vocabulary.

Everything else is held fixed: training steps, batch size, width, and the sampler search. Every model is tuned over the same five samplers on validation, and only the winner is scored on the test split. All 27 models were trained and tuned with the same protocol, on the same hardware.

### Does self conditioning change how the model should be sampled?

#### The question

The text8 result looks like SC hurting discrete models. But each model above used whichever sampler won on validation, so its score mixes two things: how good the model is, and which sampler it can use. Does SC change which sampler works best? If it does, part of the text8 result is about sampling, not about the model.

#### The experiment

This needs no new training. Every model in the previous experiment was already tuned with all five samplers, so we can read off which sampler won for each of the 27 models, and how every sampler scored on validation. Then we compare the 8-layer, SC, and 16-layer models again, with every model held to the same sampler (native). Everything here uses validation scores; the test split is still used only once per model, in the previous experiment.

<RunConfig id="sc-sampler-choice" client:visible />

#### Results

The next figure shows which sampler won for each model.

<SamplerChoice client:visible />

On text8, renoising wins for every Gaussian, masked, and uniform model without SC. With SC, renoising stops working: the masked SC model drops to under 1 valid word per sample with renoising, compared with 13.6 with the native sampler. So the SC models fall back on the native sampler, while their no-SC rivals keep the renoising boost.

If we restrict every model to the native sampler, the text8 picture flips. On validation, SC beats both the 8- and 16-layer masked models (13.6 against 11.5 and 12.4), and lands between them for uniform (14.3 against 13.6 and 14.7). In other words, much of the "SC hurts discrete models" result on text8 is really "SC models cannot use the renoising sampler."

We do not yet know why. One guess is that renoising throws away the noisy state the SC feedback was built from, so the feedback no longer matches the state the model sees. That is untested.

#### How sure are we?

The sampler flip is clear in the validation numbers, but it comes from one task and one seed. It is a good reason to never compare SC and no-SC models under a single fixed sampler.

### Does self conditioning help every continuous method?

#### The question

The experiments above use one continuous method, Gaussian corruption of learned embeddings. The codebase has three more: simplex, spherical, and vMF. Each corrupts a different representation, so if SC's benefit depends on how Gaussian noise acts on embeddings, it might not carry over. Does SC help all four?

#### The experiment

We train each of the four continuous methods with and without SC on all three tasks. That is 24 models, all with the same 8-layer backbone and the CDCD learned clock, and all tuned with the same five samplers. The same runs feed the [noise schedule example](#where-do-learned-noise-schedules-spend-training) further down.

#### Results

Each pair below is the same 8-layer model and the same learned clock, with and without SC.

<CdcdPerformance client:visible />

SC helps every continuous method on Sudoku and TinyStories. On text8 the picture is mixed. SC helps simplex most (11.5 to 13.3 valid words) and Gaussian (13.0 to 14.1). Spherical and vMF barely move (13.0 to 13.0, and 12.8 to 13.0). That is the sampler effect from [the sampling question](#does-self-conditioning-change-how-the-model-should-be-sampled) again: without SC, both pick renoising, which lifts them by 3.5 to 4 valid words on validation. With the native sampler only, SC is worth about 3 for both (9.6 to 12.3 for spherical, 9.1 to 12.8 for vMF, on validation). On TinyStories BPE, SC helps simplex most under standard BLEU, from 6.3 to 8.5. Most of that jump is length: without SC, simplex ends its stories at 80% of the real length, and without the length penalty the gain shrinks to 8.1 to 8.5. Simplex with SC scores 8.5 either way. That makes it the strongest diffusion model here, ahead of spherical with SC (8.3, or 8.5 without the penalty) and Gaussian with SC (7.9). The autoregressive model of the same size scores 8.9 (9.0 without the penalty).

SC also changes which samplers these models can use. Entropy-bounded sampling [@benhamu2025accelerated] was designed for masked diffusion: at each step it commits the positions whose predicted distribution has low entropy, up to a bound. Our EB greedy policy applies the same rule to every method, committing argmax tokens, even though the continuous models were never trained to commit tokens. With SC, simplex, spherical and vMF all work with it. On validation it comes within 0.3 to 1.1 of the native sampler on text8 and TinyStories, and beats native for spherical on TinyStories (7.85 against 7.27). Without SC it mostly breaks: spherical drops to 1.88 on text8 and 0.37 on TinyStories. Gaussian embeddings fail with it on text either way.

#### How sure are we?

The gains are large compared with sampling-seed noise and hold across tasks, but each comes from one training seed. The EB comparison uses validation scores, and we did not test EB with sampled rather than argmax tokens. The text8 result for spherical and vMF depends on the sampler, as above. The Sudoku models overfit their training puzzles, but that only affects their learned clocks (see the [noise schedule example](#where-do-learned-noise-schedules-spend-training)). The scores here are measured on held-out test puzzles.

## More worked examples

The four questions below use the same pipeline. They are collapsed to keep the post short; each opens with its main result.

### Does the tokenizer change the picture?

Does SC's effect survive the move from bytes to a 10k-token BPE vocabulary? It does: with the native sampler, SC raises all 15 method-and-vocabulary cells, though how much it helps depends on the sampler and the metric.

<ExpandableSection title="The tokenizer ladder in detail" client:load>

Most language models use subword tokenizers, while most small diffusion experiments use characters or bytes. A mechanism that depends on how hard it is to identify a noisy token could easily change with vocabulary size: with bytes, a noisy position has 260 candidates; with a 10k BPE vocabulary, it has 10,000, mostly whole words. A larger vocabulary makes every model better, so that is not the question. The question is whether SC's effect, and the differences between methods, survive the change.

We repeat the comparison on TinyStories with BPE vocabularies of 260 (bytes), 500, 1k, 5k, and 10k tokens. At each size we train Gaussian, masked, and uniform diffusion, each with and without SC, plus an autoregressive (AR) model of the same size as a reference. That is 7 models per vocabulary and 35 in total. The vocabulary is the only thing that changes along the ladder; the sequence length is set per vocabulary so every story fits. Every diffusion model is tuned with the same five samplers, and AR chooses between sampling and greedy decoding.

<RunConfig id="tokenizer-ladder" client:visible />

{/* MOCKUP: tokenizer ladder. Data in src/data/vocab_ladder.json (portable-v2-mi210 + ladder-nosc archives). */}

<VocabLadder client:visible />

Across the ladder:

- **SC's effect is visible at every vocabulary size, for every method.** With the native sampler, SC raises all 15 method and vocabulary cells. The biggest gains are Gaussian's and uniform's at small vocabularies (+1.9 BLEU at 260 tokens); masked gains a steadier 0.4 to 0.9.
- **Whether the effect shrinks depends on how you measure it.** With the native sampler on validation, Gaussian's SC gain shrinks from +1.9 at 260 tokens to +0.4 at 10k, and uniform's from +1.9 to +0.7. On the test split, with each model's chosen sampler and no length penalty, Gaussian's gain stays near +1 at every size (+0.7 to +1.1). The judge model below sides with the second reading.
- **The best sampler depends on the method.** Gaussian needs sampled clean estimates: EB greedy scores near zero, and greedy renoising trails far behind. Masked does best with native and loses 0.5 to 1.3 BLEU with sampled renoising. Uniform is the only method where EB greedy works, and it wins 5 of the 10 uniform cells.
- **AR still leads.** It sits 0.9 to 1.5 BLEU above the best diffusion model at every vocabulary size.

These bullets use validation BLEU with the length penalty, because that is what the samplers were tuned on. The chosen sampler's test score can also be shown without the penalty (**Compare methods → Chosen · test**), and two things change there. Masked, which writes the shortest stories, becomes the strongest diffusion method in 7 of the 10 vocabulary-and-SC comparisons. And the gap to AR shrinks: masked with SC comes within 0.1 BLEU of AR at 260 tokens and passes it at 1k (9.2 against 9.1).

So SC does not stop mattering at realistic vocabulary sizes. What it fixes does seem to change, though, and BLEU is too coarse to show it. The judge model below makes it visible.

**How sure are we?** The direction of the SC effect is solid: it holds in all 15 cells. Its size, and whether it shrinks with vocabulary, depends on the sampler and the metric, and comes from one training seed. The TinyStories gaps between methods are often within half a BLEU point.

</ExpandableSection>

### A second opinion from a judge model

BLEU rewards length and cannot tell a story that wanders off from one that stays on track. A judge model that reads the stories mostly agrees with BLEU without the length penalty, and shows that what SC fixes changes with the vocabulary: garbled words at 260 tokens, real words in the wrong place at 10k.

<ExpandableSection title="The judge model in detail" client:load>

BLEU compares word sequences with one real ending, and as the note on BLEU shows, it also rewards length. It cannot tell a story that wanders off from one that stays on track. Does a judge that reads the stories see the same ladder as BLEU, and can it show what SC actually fixes?

So we tried a different kind of evaluator. Using a language model as a judge of generated text is now common [@zheng2023judging; @liu2023geval]: the judge reads an output and answers a question about it, such as which of two responses is better or how coherent a passage is. The usual catch is that the judge answers in free text, so its verdict has to be parsed out of the response, and an answer in the wrong format has to be retried or dropped. [Jev](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/system-one), a model from TypeSafe, is an LLM judge without that step. It answers typed questions (yes or no, pick one of several, place on a scale) and returns each answer as a probability over the allowed options. Nothing needs to be extracted, and every answer comes with a confidence [@li2026jevjudge].

<div className="callout">
<span className="callout-label">Disclosure</span>

[Jev](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/system-one) is made by TypeSafe. We are not sponsored by them.

</div>

We were mostly playing with it as a tool for evaluating language, and two things made it useful. First, it lets us break "is this a good story?" into narrow questions and ask each one separately. Second, it is fast and cheap enough to ask a lot of them: about 160,000 judgments of the ladder's test stories (512 per model) took under 15 minutes and cost under $10. We asked:

- **Best of three.** Given an opening and three endings from Gaussian, masked, and uniform diffusion (all with SC, or all without), which ending is best for coherence and staying on topic?
- **SC or not.** The same method with and without SC: which ending is better?
- **Real or model.** The model's ending next to the real one: which did the original author write? 50% would mean Jev cannot tell them apart.
- **Ratings.** Coherence and fluency on a fixed scale. The real endings are rated the same way, so scores can be read relative to them.
- **Garbled words.** One yes or no question per distinct word: is it misspelled, made up, a fragment, or two words fused?

Every pairwise question was asked twice, with the order of the options swapped, and the answers averaged. No new models are trained: Jev reads the test stories of the 35 ladder models above.

<JevLadder client:visible />

Jev's view of the ladder:

- **For Gaussian, SC's effect does not fade with vocabulary.** Jev prefers Gaussian's SC version 77 to 85% of the time at every size (**SC vs no SC**). For masked and uniform, the preference eases at larger vocabularies, to 63% at 10k. (Uniform at 1k, at 48%, looks like a weak checkpoint on every measure.)
- **What SC fixes changes with vocabulary.** At 260 tokens, Gaussian without SC writes 6.4 garbled words per 100, and SC cuts that to 1.9. At 10k there are few garbled words left to fix (0.5 per 100), yet SC's gain in realism, relative to AR, is four times larger than at 260 (+0.12 against +0.03). Once tokens are whole words, Gaussian's mistakes become real words in the wrong place, and that is what SC repairs.
- **Without SC, Gaussian is the only method that falls further behind AR as the vocabulary grows.** Relative to AR at the same vocabulary size, the rate at which Jev mistakes it for the original author drops from 0.31 to 0.22 (**Mistaken for the author**, **÷ AR at same vocab**). Masked and uniform without SC hold steady, and Gaussian with SC stays between 0.28 and 0.35.
- **It agrees with BLEU without the length penalty, not with standard BLEU.** Its pick for the best method matches the penalty-free ranking in 7 of the 10 vocabulary-and-SC comparisons, and the standard ranking in 2. Masked comes out first or close to it; Gaussian comes out last.
- **AR is far ahead, by much more than BLEU shows.** Jev mistakes AR's endings for the original author's 34 to 45% of the time, against 9 to 20% for the diffusion models. Head to head, a diffusion model beats AR at most 12% of the time.

**How sure are we?** Jev is one judge, and we have not checked it against human ratings. Some of its yes or no questions misfire on the real stories: it flags 32% of real endings as repetitive, so we do not report that flag. When it picks among five or more options, it slightly favors the middle position, so we only report two- and three-way comparisons. And every model here is still one training run. We read these results as a second opinion that points the same way as BLEU without the length penalty, not as ground truth.

</ExpandableSection>

### Where do learned noise schedules spend training?

Every continuous recipe here learns its noise schedule with CDCD. The learned clocks do make the loss climb linearly, but they spend most of training where a token's identity is already gone while its rank is not, and SC barely moves them.

<ExpandableSection title="The noise schedules in detail" client:load>

Schedules matter more here than for masked diffusion, where the schedule mostly changes the variance of training, not the objective [@Sahoo_Arriola_Schiff_Gokaslan_Marroquin_Chiu_Rush_Kuleshov_2024]. For continuous methods, the schedule decides how much training time goes to each noise level, and a bad choice can waste most of it. Every continuous recipe here learns its schedule with CDCD [@dieleman2022continuous]. CDCD fits the expected loss as a function of noise level and samples training times through its inverse, so that every stretch of the warped time $u$ carries the same share of the loss. Because the clock adapts to the model's own loss, SC could move it.

We measure three things on the 24 continuous models from the self conditioning section: whether loss climbs linearly in $u$, what the learned clocks look like, and how quickly each clock destroys a token's identity and its rank. The schedule figures use text8 and byte-level TinyStories checkpoints from an earlier training run of the same recipes, on different GPUs.

**Does the clock do its job?** If the clock is right, measured loss should climb in a straight line in $u$.

<CdcdLinearity client:visible />

On both text tasks, it does. A straight line explains at least 99.8% of the variation in the measured loss, on training data and on held-out data, for every method. On Sudoku, it does not, even on training data. The Sudoku models overfit their training puzzles, so their clocks do not describe held-out loss. We keep them behind a toggle for completeness.

**How sure are we?** The check on text is solid: the line fits for every method on both text tasks, on training and on held-out data. Each clock comes from one training seed. The Sudoku clocks do not describe held-out loss, so we leave them out of every claim about the clocks. Their test scores are unaffected.

**Where does it spend training?** Each line below is the schedule CDCD learned for one method on one task.

<CdcdSchedules client:visible />

The raw noise parameters are hard to compare across methods, since a noise level of 0.5 means something different for a Gaussian embedding than for a point on the simplex. So the next figure measures corruption in two units every method shares: how often the nearest embedding is the wrong token, and where the true token ranks among all candidates.

<CdcdCorruption client:visible />

The two measures disagree sharply. On text8, the nearest token is already wrong more than half the time within the first 12% of the learned clock. Yet the true token's rank is still only halfway to chance at about 75% of the clock for Gaussian, spherical, and vMF, and at about 95% for simplex. On byte-level TinyStories, the gap is even wider. So the learned clocks spend most of training at noise levels where the token's identity is gone, but its rank is not.

This matches the picture of token identifiability from CANDI [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], where Gaussian noise destroys a token's identity and its rank at very different rates. Here, the learned clocks arrive at that split on their own.

**How sure are we?** The split between identity and rank holds for every method on both text tasks. Each clock comes from one training seed, and these checkpoints come from an earlier training run than the scores above.

**Does SC move the clock?** The clock is adaptive: it is fit to the model's own loss, so anything that changes how hard each noise level is for the model should move it. SC gives the model its previous prediction at every step, which could plausibly make high noise levels easier. Figure I draws each clock with SC (solid) and without (dashed). On the text tasks the two nearly coincide: the point where rank information runs out moves by about 0.02 in $u$. This is a little puzzling next to the scores, where SC changes most methods a lot (see [Does self conditioning help every continuous method?](#does-self-conditioning-help-every-continuous-method)). Whatever SC fixes, it does not show up as a change in where the loss is spent.

**How sure are we?** The small effect of SC on the clocks holds for every method on both text tasks. Each clock comes from one training seed, and the clocks and the scores come from two separate training runs of the same recipes.

</ExpandableSection>

### Scale the encoder or the decoder?

In encoder-decoder block diffusion, which side is worth making deeper? The decoder. Decoder depth drives quality, while 1 to 8 encoder layers stay within sampling-seed noise, and one shared transformer does as well as the split at standard BLEU.

<ExpandableSection title="Block diffusion in detail" client:load>

Block diffusion generates a sequence one block at a time. Each block is denoised while the model reads the blocks already written. Ordinary block diffusion [@arriola2025block] uses one transformer for both jobs. E2D2 [@arriola2025e2d2] splits them: an encoder reads the clean context, and a decoder denoises the current block. The encoder runs once per block, while the decoder runs at every denoising step. So the two sides have very different costs, and we can ask which one is worth making deeper.

The design map above shows how the weights are split: pick block diffusion, then **Weights → Encoder + decoder (E2D2)** under Sequence modifiers.

To separate the two sides, we vary one while holding the other fixed. We train E2D2 with the decoder fixed at 8 layers and the encoder at 1, 2, 4, or 8, and with the encoder fixed at 8 and the decoder at 1, 2, 4, or 8. Two more models bound the comparison: one with no encoder at all, where the decoder reads the raw context embeddings, and ordinary block diffusion with one shared 8-layer transformer. That is 9 models per task and 27 in total, which took about 233 GPU hours of training (6 to 14 hours per run). All of them are masked block diffusion with blocks of 32 tokens and no SC, tuned with the native samplers only.

<RunConfig id="block-encoder-decoder" client:visible />

{/* MOCKUP: block diffusion figure. Data in src/data/block_scaling.json (portable-v2-mi210 snapshot). */}

<BlockEncDec client:visible />

In short:

- **Decoder depth matters.** On text8, going from 1 to 8 decoder layers raises the score from 5.6 to 9.8. On TinyStories, it rises from 6.2 to 7.0, with most of the gain by 4 layers (7.7 to 8.8 without the length penalty, with all of the gain by 2 layers).
- **Encoder depth does not.** With an 8-layer decoder, 1 to 8 encoder layers stay within sampling-seed noise on both tasks. On text8, even no encoder at all scores the same.
- **One encoder layer is enough here.** On TinyStories, the first encoder layer helps over raw embeddings (6.5 to 7.0, or 7.8 to 8.7 without the length penalty). Layers after that add nothing.
- **Splitting does not beat sharing, at least by standard BLEU.** One shared 8-layer transformer matches E2D2 8/8 on both tasks, with half the layers. On TinyStories this depends on the metric: the shared model writes longer stories than any E2D2 model (88% of the real length against 79 to 82%), and without the length penalty E2D2 8/8 is clearly ahead (8.8 against 7.9).
- **Encoder layers are cheap at inference; decoder layers are not.** Inference time barely changes with encoder depth, but more than doubles from 1 to 8 decoder layers on TinyStories (18 to 44 seconds).

At this scale, the practical advice is simple. Put the layers in the decoder, keep the encoder shallow, and expect a single shared transformer to do just as well. The encoder only needs to summarize context that is already clean, which may be an easy job at these sequence lengths.

**How sure are we?** The decoder trend is solid: the gaps are far larger than sampling-seed noise. The encoder result is suggestive. These block models were tuned with the native samplers only, come from one training seed, and use short sequences. Longer contexts could easily give the encoder more to do. The Sudoku block models barely learned the task, so we leave them out of every claim.

</ExpandableSection>

## Takeaways

The argument of this post is the modular view. Split diffusion language models into parts that can be swapped across recipes, and use that to measure how the parts interact rather than assume they do not. The experiments are small examples of what that makes possible, and several of their most useful results are interactions.

- **Self conditioning is more than extra depth, for Gaussian corruption.** An 8-layer model with SC beats a 16-layer model without it on all three tasks, without adding a parameter.
- **SC and the sampler interact.** On text8, models without SC do best with renoising, while models with SC cannot use it. Comparing SC and no-SC models under one fixed sampler can reverse the result.
- **SC does not help every method equally.** It improves all four continuous methods (Gaussian, simplex, spherical and vMF) on Sudoku and TinyStories. For masked diffusion its effect is modest at best: it lowers Sudoku accuracy from 96.9% to 92.6%, and its TinyStories gains are small.
- **The effect survives larger vocabularies.** From 260 to 10k BPE tokens, SC improves every method at every size. What it fixes changes: at small vocabularies it removes garbled words; at large ones it puts real words in the right place.
- **A clock that is linear in loss is not linear in corruption.** CDCD's learned clocks spend most of training where a token's identity is gone but its rank is not. For Gaussian, spherical and vMF, the true token's rank is halfway to chance at about 75% of the clock; for simplex, at about 95%. SC barely moves the clocks.
- **In block diffusion, spend layers on the decoder.** With an 8-layer decoder, encoder depth from 1 to 8 layers stays within sampling-seed noise, and one shared 8-layer transformer matches E2D2 8/8 under standard BLEU. Encoder layers are cheap at inference, and decoder layers are not [@arriola2025e2d2].

### Limitations

These claims come with clear limits:

- **Scale.** Every model is small (8 layers of width 512, or 16 for the depth comparison) and trained for 20,000 steps on small tasks. Whether the results hold at realistic scale is an open question.
- **One seed, shared training settings.** Each cell is a single training run. Training hyperparameters are shared across methods rather than tuned for each one; only the inference settings were tuned, on validation data.
- **The tasks are not ordinary language modeling.** In Sudoku, the nine digits appear equally often in every solution, while language tokens follow a heavily skewed, Zipf-like distribution. text8 and TinyStories are small and simple corpora.
- **The metrics are coarse.** BLEU rewards length, and the judge model is a single judge we have not checked against human ratings.
- **The runs used an earlier internal version of the code.** Each experiment is a [worked example](https://github.com/patrickpynadath1/sddc/tree/383616271af4bb858cdba117387dc24928ad7a58/experiments) in the public repo with the same protocol, so a rerun should give close numbers, not identical ones.

## Future directions

This project will keep changing as we find new questions to ask. The codebase also covers a narrow slice of current DLM research (see [the scope](#scope-what-is-this-codebase)). These are the directions where we would most welcome contributions.

### Hybrid methods

Every experiment in this post uses a single corruption process. That leaves out one of the most promising directions: hybrids that corrupt the same tokens with a discrete and a continuous process at once [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion; @zheng2025continuously; @davis2026design; @zhou2025coevolutionary]. The codebase already supports a large space of them. A hybrid is a set of views of the same tokens, each with its own representation, corruption process and noise schedule, read by one shared network. Any discrete view (masked or uniform) can be paired with any continuous one (Gaussian embeddings, simplex, spherical or vMF), and more than two views can be combined. Hybrids can also be layered rather than placed side by side. SemiCat [@davis2026design] combines all three kinds of corruption: a Gaussian interpolant wrapped around a corruption on the simplex, plus discrete reveals that switch each position to its clean token at a random time. That construction is not in the codebase yet, but it fits the same split into information path, denoising task and inference. None of these combinations is studied here. Which pairings work best, and whether the results above (SC beating depth, the sampler flip, where learned clocks spend training) carry over to hybrids, are open questions the codebase can already answer.

### Sampler distillation

Distillation, consistency and flow map methods [@roos2026categoricalflowmaps; @lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous] are among the most active areas of DLM research, and the codebase leaves them out because they train on the model's own trajectories. SDDC treats many recipes as settings of one skeleton. A shared framework of the same kind for distillation methods would make them far easier to compare.

### Insertion and deletion

The codebase can insert and delete tokens, including a FlexMDM-style recipe [@kim2026any], but the implementation is basic. There is much more room to explore how these modifiers interact with the rest of a recipe, and to make them work well rather than merely work.

### Latent diffusion and other representations

Latent diffusion for language has made real progress [@lovelace2023latent; @meshchaninov2026cosmoscompressedsmoothlatent; @shen2026codar]. Bringing compressed and contextual latent spaces into the same framework, along with representations that split tokens into smaller units, such as CoBit's bitstreams [@batzolis2026towards], would show which of the results here depend on per-token representations.

These are only a few directions, and we are sure we are missing others.

More compute would buy three things: several training seeds per cell, so every comparison gets error bars; larger models and longer training, to see which results survive scale; and realistic vocabularies of 32k to 50k tokens.
