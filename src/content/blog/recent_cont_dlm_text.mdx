## Introduction 
While autoregressive language modeling is currently the dominant paradigm, alternative paradigms have seen significant progress in recent years. This motivates foundational research into pretraining such models to better understand the optimal design choices within these paradigms. 
Openwebtext and GPT-2 scale is the de-facto norm for studying pretraining, as it is large and varied enough to reflect the nature of pretraining data while being practical to train on an academic budget. 
<br />

However, this still leaves the question of evaluation: the existing evaluation framework for this scale of models is based on likelihood evaluations, but this becomes questionable when different paradigms present fundementally different ELBOs. 
In this respect, generation based metrics are very useful, as all that is required is to be able to use the pretrained model to generate samples, and measure how language-like the samples are via some external model. 
<br />
Generative perplexity still has its own issues. In this blog, we focus on its sensitivity to sample entropy, which is a measure of token diversity. 
Generative frontiers are a useful tool to address this issue: instead of displaying the results for one temperature, we perform a sweep across a range of temperatures and display the pareto frontier for each method in terms of entropy and perplexity. 
<br />

We can use this to observe that model performance is often quite good early on in training. We compare the frontiers for MDLM, Duo, CANDI, and FLM and observe that the frontiers at 50k training steps are close to the final generative frontiers after 1,000,000 steps. 
Finally, we also use these frontiers to discuss recent progress in continuous denoising models, as reflected through the recent FLM paper, which proposes a time reparameterization to be linear in terms of discrete corruption instead of flow time. 
We end on how pure continuous denoising models offer a compelling direction for few step generation that may not be attainable under a pure discrete framework.

## Pretraining: Why OWT is a Meaningful Dataset 

Pretraining language models serves a substantial test for new paradigms on language modeling -- it is often the case that methods that seem like they should work do not work, and vice versa. 
However, pretraining language models at the scale of a billion parameters or more is usually less feasible for academic budgets -- even a GPT-2 size model takes around a week of pretraining on a dedicated node of 8 H100 GPUs. 
Thus the scale of GPT-2 models seems to be the standard benchmark used for studying pretraining of alternative paradigms for language modeling beyond autoregressive. Specifically, it is common to pretrain a ~150 million parameter model with a diffusion transformer architecture on OpenWebText [@Lou_Meng_Ermon_2024] [@Sahoo_Arriola_Schiff_Gokaslan_Marroquin_Chiu_Rush_Kuleshov_2024] [@Shi_Han_Wang_Doucet_Titsias_2024] [@arriola2025block]. 
This is the standard for a reason -- it is usually close to the edge of what is attainable on an academic budget, but is still similar enough to pretraining data to obtain meaningful performance metrics. 
<br />
<ExpandableSection title="On Smaller Datasets" client:load>
While there are smaller datasets such as LM1B that can be used for training language models, it is important to note that these datasets often differ from pretraining data in substantial ways. **In particular, LM1B is a of tokens from news sources shuffled at a sentence level** [@chelba2014billionwordbenchmarkmeasuring].  As a result, there is no notion of coherence across different sentences, since the ordering is fundamentally random. This was intentional -- this benchmark was introduced in 2013, when neural networks were still being compared against n-gram models. Even at the time of introduction, it was primarily intended for likelihood based evaluation -- it was designed to measure whether neural networks could properly model language at the sentence level better than n-gram models. For demonstrating language ability beyond generating random sentences, LM1B is not an ideal dataset for pretraining -- this is why most diffusion papers language model papers use LM1B for only likelihood or zero shot likelihood evaluation [@Sahoo_Arriola_Schiff_Gokaslan_Marroquin_Chiu_Rush_Kuleshov_2024] [@arriola2025block] [@sahoo2025diffusion] [@Shi_Han_Wang_Doucet_Titsias_2024].

</ExpandableSection>
## Generative Perplexity
While the question of what scale to train models at and what data to use is a fairly closed topic, there still remains the evaluation problem. While the original GPT-2 paper demonstrates that pretraining on web-text enabled zero-shot task performance on a wide range of tasks, it is crucial to note that the performance usually required scaling up the number of parameters -- which is difficult when ~150 million is already quite expensive. Furthermore, some of the primary results are zero-shot likelihood across different unseen datasets across different models to demonstrate scaling behavior [@radford2021learning]. This was useful as all models were autoregressive, and thus measured likelihood in the exact same. More intuitively, all the likelihoods measured the same ability -- how well the model could predict the next token, given the context. With different ELBO formulations, comparing the likelihoods directly seems questionable [@theis2016noteevaluationgenerativemodels]. 
<br />

<ExpandableSection title="Why Language Understanding Tasks Aren't Sufficient for Generative Models" client:load>

It is worth mentioning that it is possible to measure model capability in terms of language understanding tasks, similar to those used in the foundational GPT-2 paper. However, it is crucial to note that good performance on these tasks do not necessarily translate into good language generative ability, which is ultimately what we want from generative language models. To see we this, we can look at the example of BERT: while the original BERT model had strong performance across natural language understanding and inference [@devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional], it was not suitable on its own for language generation, despite attempts to enable it to serve as a generative model [@wang2019bertmouthspeakbert]. It wasn't until advances in masked diffusion that these models became viable for generative tasks [@Sahoo_Arriola_Schiff_Gokaslan_Marroquin_Chiu_Rush_Kuleshov_2024] [@Shi_Han_Wang_Doucet_Titsias_2024] [@ou2025absorbingdiscretediffusionsecretly]. Thus strong performance on language understanding tasks is not sufficient evidence of generative quality — which is ultimately what we want to measure for generative language models.

</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Why Generative Perplexity has become standard" client:load>
For this reason, generative perplexity or generative negative log likelihood has gradually become a core tool for comparing different models [@zheng2024masked] [@dieleman2022continuous] [@sahoo2025diffusion] [@sahoo2025esoteric]. All that is required for generative perplexity is the ability to sample from a trained model -- afterwards, we simply run a pre-trained autoregressive model such as GPT-2 Large on the generations and report the likelihoods. We do this for the following reasons:
<br />

1) AR models compute the likelihoods in a single forward pass, and 
2) GPT-2 was trained on web-text, which is the original inspiration for the OpenWebText used widely for pre-training language models. 
</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Generative Perplexity as Data Likelihood Oracle" client:load>

We can view GPT-2 large as an oracle for likelihoods from the target distribution, to the extent that GPT-2 has successfully learned the target distribution. 

While there are other metrics for evaluating generations, generative perplexity seems to be used more frequently because it is easier to interpret. For example, MAUVE is another metric that is used to measure generation quality [@meshchaninov2025compressed]. However, this metric is not as interpretable (it represents a KL divergence based on extracted hidden representations from both reference and generated text), still dependent on having some oracle model, and was originally designed for comparisons for conditional generation [@pillutla2021mauvemeasuringgapneural]. It also requires a fairly large sample size to get meaningful results. Since temperature and NFE can affect sample quality, this can be quite costly. 
<br />

</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Caveats to Generative Perplexity" client:load>
It is important to note that this interpretation depends on the following: 
<br />
1) How well GPT-2 models its training data, WebText
2) How close the training data for the target language model aligns with WebText. 

More concretely, if the target model is trained on a dataset different than WebText or uses a different tokenizer, then perplexity can no longer be reasonably interpreted as a proxy for data likelihood. 

In this case, perplexity would reflect some combination of how close the target model can capture its training data, and how close its training data is to WebText. These two factors may have some interdependent effect on the resulting likelihood from the oracle model, and thus it is more difficult to interpret the resulting perplexity values. In the scenario where the training data differs substantially from WebText, it is probably more suitable to use MAUVE, since it is designed to compare against a reference set of text. 
</ExpandableSection>
## Generative Frontiers: Why They Are Necessary for Accurate Analysis

### Perplexity Sensitivity to Entropy
By definition, perplexity is a measure of how predictable tokens are in a given sequence. Thus if the unigram distribution of a sentence is more degenerate / has mass more concentrated on specific words, it will have a better perplexity value, as the oracle model can simply increase the mass placed on high frequency words throughout its predictions. This does not reflect better language quality necessarily -- it just reflects a more predictable unigram distribution. 

This has already been observed in [@zheng2024masked], where they demonstrate that even differing logit precisions can result in misleading perplexity values that are revealed via entropy. However, the sensitivity of perplexity of to entropy goes beyond just severe mode collapse -- **even slight changes in entropy can result in more competitive perplexity metrics.** 

To demonstrate this, we plot generative frontiers -- which show entropy and perplexity values attainable for each model via temperature tuning. We observe that while the points are on the same frontiers -- the model ability remains the same -- the single-point comparisons can change, depending on where we pick points on the frontier. 
![Entropy ranking demo](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/entropy_ranking_demo.gif)
<br />
<ExpandableSection title="Role of Temperature Tuning in Language modeling" client:load>
Temperature and other inference-time adjustments to logits is already standard practice in autoregressive models, and even larger scale diffusion language models. However, when looking at the literature that studies diffusion LMs at the scale of GPT-2, it is common practice to report a single performance point at either a universal inference setting for all models, or the temperature of 1.0. 

Even if Model A outperforms Model B at temperature 1.0, if Model B can be tuned to beat Model A by lowering the temperature -- which does not require any retraining -- is it really fair to claim Model A is better than Model B? 

</ExpandableSection>
To further emphasize that single point comparisons are mutable, depending on where we pick the points we pick from the frontier, we created an interactive app. The user can slide along the frontier for each method and generate their own rankings for the given models. 
The differing rankings are not contradictory -- they are merely different slices of the same generative frontier.

### Interpeting Generative Frontiers Generative Frontiers
For this reason, frontiers of entropy v.s perplexity, as introduced in [@zheng2024masked] and expanded on later in [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], are a very useful tool. These frontiers address the sensitivity of perplexity to entropy by revealing the full frontier, which allows for stronger conclusions. If a model outperforms another at a fixed temperature, this only reflects performance at that temperature and is mutable depending on tuning. If a model dominates another in terms of entropy-perplexity frontiers, this means that no amount of tuning (at least for temperatures) can result in better perplexity without sacrificing diversity

<ExpandableSection title="Why Frontiers are not standard practice" client:load>

It is understandable why frontier analysis is not often used — it requires extensive temperature sweeps, and must be repeated for each NFE, and is also not standard practice within the field. Additionally, it results in more nuanced results that may not reveal a clear winner — there may be regions of entropy where one model outperforms  and another where it underperforms. But such complexity isn’t a result of the metric, but rather the task itself — we are trying to evaluate how language-like generations are, and what constitutes good writing can vary based on context, reader, etc. The field has shifted towards using perplexity to capture a quality that is very multi-faceted, so it makes sense that resulting comparisons are also multi-faceted.

</ExpandableSection>
### What defines a reasonable entropy

It is natural to wonder what a reasonable entropy is -- if a model outperforms another at a very low entropy, this outperformance may not translate directly into better generations if the entropy is too far from actual language entropy. Thus a reasonable approach is to empirically measure the entropy across the validation set of openwebtext and use it to define intervals across the entropy scale. We can then fix this entropy value and look at the resulting perplexity values for various methods, plotting the perplexity against NFE. This gives a clear picture of how models perform when within a reasonable range of entropy, since this distribution is directly based off of natural language. We include an interactive app that can be used to slide along the empirical entropy distribution and visualize the entropy-corrected NFE v.s PPL plots.

## Interesting Side Note: 50k steps is sometimes good enough 
We can use frontier analysis to observe something that may be of interest to those working on pretraining -- often, the generative performance of models very early on in training (50k) steps is quite close to the model's ability after 1,000,000 steps. We visualize this below by pretraining Duo, MDLM, and CANDI for 50k steps and comparing against the frontiers generated by fully trained checkpoints. as one can observe, they are remarkably close. Thus when iterating on an algorithm / training framework, it is most likely more than sufficient to use 50k steps -- if a model does not work by this point, it will probably not work by 1,000,000 steps. 
<br />
It is also worth noting that this may indicate a limitation of either generative perplexity or this scale of models in terms of measuring model performance -- it is a commonly known fact that training for longer improves performance on downstream tasks. It is not known whether the above saturation is a result of model capacity or lack of perplexity to capture fine-grained differences in model capability.

## Base Performance of Continuous Denoising Models v.s Discrete and Hybrid 

Using frontier analysis, we can examine some recent progress with continuous denoising models -- specifically, the flow-matching based framework introduced by [@roos2026categoricalflowmaps] and [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous]. We focus on FLM [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous] as they provide results for pretraining on OWT. 
<ExpandableSection title="Experimental Setup" client:load>
We use a ~160 million parameter model trained for 50k steps on OWT for preliminary frontier comparisons when comparing against the continuous baseline from CANDI. When comparing against frontiers for Duo, CANDI, and MDLM, we use the official reported metrics from the FLM paper. These are fair comparisons, as all models use a diffusion transformer architecture, are trained on OpenWebText for up to 1 million steps, and have around the same model size.and use the official numbers from their paper for comparisons against fully trained frontiers. 
</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Background on CANDI and FLM" client:load>
In CANDI, we argued that continuous language models tend to underperform because of a problematic scaling with discrete corruption with continuous noise level at large vocabulary. We use this to motivate decoupling discrete and continuous corruption through a hybrid corruption process. In FLM, the authors build on the observation that discrete corruption is ill behaved with respect to continuous noise level or time of the flow matching / diffusion process. However, instead of decoupling the process, they propose to reparameterize time to be linear with respect to the discrete corruption rate. They argue that continuous denoising models make for superior base models than alternative approaches, such as pure discrete or hybrid. They then propose a distillation framework that enables few / single step generation. 
</ExpandableSection>

### FLM Is Substantial Progress for Continuous Denoising
The time reparameterization does substantially improve performance of the model in terms of generative. We train an FLM base model for 50k steps and compare it against the pure one-hot diffusion model from CANDI, which is trained on one-hots noised with a VE schedule. This diffusion baseline is also parameterized via softmax on the logits, and trained with cross entropy. we put the results below. 
![FLM v Cont](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/flm_v_cont.png)

<br />
As observed, FLM is order of magnitude better than the continuous baseline -- the y axis is on a log scale, so the outperformance is quite dramatic. The original conclusion after the experiment in CANDI was that continuous diffusion is fundementally broken at high vocabularies. This is clearly not the case -- the reparameterization results in a reasonable base language model. This is clear evidence that the time reparameterization proposed in FLM is unlocking something crucial for continuous language models.

We can explain the underperformance of FLM against discrete and hybrid methods by observing the animations of the different noising denoising processes. More precisely, we can compare the noise schedule of FLM against 
the noise schedule of Candi to understand why it underperforms. We include the original figures from both CANDI and FLM below.

As visible in both graphs, discrete corruption is ill behaved with continuous noise and flow time. In FLM, the time reparameterization is motivated by the goal of ensuring that progress within the schedule contributes uniformly to decorruption when moving from noise to clean signal. We include an animation of the a normal flow matching schedule simulated on a vocabulary of 50,000 words to illustrate this -- observe that most of the discrete decorruption occurs at the very end of the process. 
![Normal FM Gif](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/normal_fm_sched.gif)

If we desire discrete decorruption to be uniform when sampling, that implies discrete corruption should be linear when noising. This is precisely the goal of CANDI's hybrid schedule -- we decouple discrete and continuous corruption from each other, and ensure that both (including discrete corruption) are linear functions with respect to time. 

<ExpandableSection title="Why Continuous Language Models need Discrete Structure" client:load>
Part of the reason both CANDI and FLM want discrete decorruption to be uniform throughout training and inference is due to the discrete nature of text. Intuitively, take the following corrupted sentence: 
```
I live in [Mask] York City. 
```
The reader will probably recognize the mask token should be "New". This is not due to "New" or "York" being semantically similar on their own, or being similar in terms of characters -- it is because "York City" is usually preceded by "New" whenever the former appears in text. In other words, the conditional relation between "York City" and "New" is something observed through data -- not through anything inherent to the words themselves. 
<br />
In contrast, take this noisy picture of two cats. 
<br /> 
![Noisy Cats](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/cats_noisy.png)
<br />
While the image is blurry, it is clear what colors the pixels should be -- the noisy pixels on the bottom should be blue, in the middle should be gray, and the top should be black. The reader could probably infer these despite never seeing these cats, or that couch beforehand. This is because images are spatially smooth -- nearby pixels tend to share similar values. Thus the sample itself has a correlational structure that is not dependent on previous data or information. (maybe explain more about rapsd, cite Sander blog). 
<br />
Thus in language, when generating a sequence, what words are reasonable for a given position depends on what are the clean words at other positions. This means that at some point, in order for the sequence to emerge, some clean words have to be determined so the other positions can be resolved conditioned on those words. Continuous hints can provide soft preferences for each position, but resolving those preferences into actual tokens requires conditioning on other resolved tokens. In images, the denoising process is tethered by correlational structure -- like in the example of the noisy cats, each pixel can look towards the neighbors to get an idea of what value it should move towards. This is why discrete decorruption — having clean anchor tokens available throughout the process — is essential for continuous language models despite not being needed for other domains.
<br />
This is supported by the fact that the FLM model we train for 50k steps outperforms the continuous counterpart -- both are trained with corruption on the one-hot space, parameterized via softmax, trained via cross entropy, and have unconstrained noise in the euclidean space. However, defining the trajectory in to be linear in terms of discrete corruption ensures that each denoising step resolves new anchor tokens, which provide conditioning signal for the remaining positions in subsequent steps.
</ExpandableSection>
### Compressed v.s Uncompressed Continuous Noising 
While both CANDI and FLM have similar motivations, the execution is different: FLM introduces a time reparameterization that moves along flow time in a way that ensures that discrete corruption is uniformly distributed across time. But since discrete corruption is concentrated within a very narrow band of noise level, the change of continuous noise with respect to time is very non-linear. We include an animation of their schedule. We observe that the discrete decorruption is evenly spread out across time. However, the continuous denoising is compressed and dilated relative to the noise schedule for standard flow matching. 

![FLM Gif](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/flm_sched.gif)
<br />

<ExpandableSection title="Why Reparameterization leads to Compressed Noise Schedule" client:load>

This compression of the continuous noise schedule is a direct consequence of the non-linear relationship between discrete corruption and continuous noise level. In standard flow matching, continuous noise is linear in time, making discrete corruption non-linear. FLM's reparameterization inverts this: discrete corruption becomes linear in time, but continuous noise becomes non-linear. The non-linearity doesn't disappear — it just moves from one axis to the other. Specifically, the early phase of the process — where continuous refinement occurs but no tokens are resolved — gets compressed into very few effective steps. Meanwhile, the narrow band of flow time where discrete corruption actually occurs gets stretched across most of the reparameterized schedule.
</ExpandableSection>
The implicit argument within the FLM reparameterization is that the the early denoising phase is not significantly important, since it does not result in the resolution of many positions. **In CANDI, we argue that denoising isn't just about picking the right token — it's about refining the continuous landscape that informs that choice**. A key advantage of continuous methods over pure discrete is the presence of soft "hints" for each position that inform the final prediction, as opposed to predicting based off of a masked token (cite CDCD, CADD, CANDI, etc). 
<br /> 
The early denoising stage before the token is stage is crucial for refining this soft hints -- in effect, it increases the value of token coordinates that are likely, and decreases the values for unlikely tokens. In order for these hints to be meaningful, there must be sufficient steps to iteratively refine these soft "guesses" -- so that when tokens are finally resolved as anchors, those decisions are well-informed by the continuous geometry. We argue that this is why CANDI is able to outperform the 1024 NFE performance of base FLM with 32 NFE. We include a visualization of the CANDI schedule below. 
![CANDI FM Gif](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/candi_sched.gif)

<ExpandableSection title="Implication of Reparameterization for Soft Refinement" client:load>

However, since the FLM schedule moves uniformly in terms of discrete corruption, and most of the continuous denoising in standard flow time has very little discrete decorruption, this stage of refinement is essentially covered in a single step. This means that the soft "guesses" are determined almost immediately in one step, causing discrete decisions based on un-refined continuous estimates. As a result, methods that do not use continuous hints altogether, such as Duo and MDLM, are able to surpass the 1024 NFE performance with only 32 steps. Furthermore, CANDI, which trains across an uncompressed noise range and thus allocates more training towards continuous denoising, also outperforms the base FLM when controlling for entropy. 
</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Tension between Discrete and Continuous Denoising in FLM" client:load>

This reveals a fundemental tension with the reparameterization approach: while the goal is to leverage continuous geometry, the reparameterization results in a schedule where most of the continuous movement is handled very coarsely, and quality depends entirely on the model's ability to discretely resolve positions. In effect, the reparameterization schedule sacrifices learning continuous geometry for learning discrete denoising. This is not a limitation of FLM's specific choice of reparameterization — it is a consequence of the nonlinear relationship between continuous noise and discrete corruption at large vocabulary sizes. Any single-process schedule must choose which axis to linearize, and the other will necessarily be compressed.
</ExpandableSection>
<ExpandableSection title="How Hybrid Methods balance Discrete and Continuous denoising" client:load>
In contrast, Hybrid Discrete-Continuous methods make it is possible to uniformly step through discrete decorruption and continuous denoising simultaneously. In fact, the core design of CANDI is to enable complete freedom over the continuous noise schedule without needing to deal with the consequences in terms of discrete corruption. While we design a continuous noise schedule based on the inuition that continuous rank degradation should be linear, any continuous schedule is possible in theory. Concurrent work CADD leverages a linear interpolation flow matching schedule, and achieves SOTA results on coding tasks with an 8b diffusion model obtained from the DiffuCoder training pipeline [@zheng2025continuously]. Hybrid methods such as CADD, CANDI, and CCDD demonstrate that tradeoff made by FLM's reparameterization is not inherent to the problem — it is an artifact of coupling discrete and continuous corruption through a single process [@zhou2025coevolutionary]. By decoupling them, hybrid methods capture the immediate benefits of continuous geometry in terms of undistilled model performance without sacrificing discrete conditioning.
</ExpandableSection>

## Distillation Performance of Continuous Denoising Models 

### FLM Achieves New Heights for Few-step Language Generation
The main contribution of FLM is not only the base model, but actually the novel distillation method proposed that enables single step / few step generation. Notably, their results for distillation do clearly demonstrate that the distilled FLM is closer to achieving single step generation when compared to discrete baselines. Here we plot the OWT results from their paper on entropy and perplexity frontiers. 
![FLM Few Step](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/flm_distill_fewstep.png)
<br />

It is notable that for few step generation, it appears that the distilled FLM achieves better entropy and perplexity than the distilled duo models. While the distilled versions of MDLM do achieve higher entropy, this does come at the cost of worse perplexity. While we do not have access to the checkpoints for distilled MDLM models, the outperformance in terms of perplexity seem significant enough for the single step regime (~1000) that temperature tuning would not change the relative rankings. However, it is not possible to make definitive statements without actual frontiers due to the entropy differences. 
<br /> 

We illustrate the progression in the few step regime of all methods. Notably, discrete methods require more steps to achieve similar few-step performance -- this supports the factorization error argument made in FLM regarding why continuous methods are better suited for distillation. 
![FLM Few-step Gif](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/owt_animated.gif)
### Limitations of Current Few-step Language Generation 
While distilled FLM achieves very strong results in the few step regime, it is worth showing the results in the context of the empirical entropy distribution of OWT. 
First, we show a visualization of a KDE estimate of the sample entropy distribution over the OWT evaluation set, which contextualizes the operating points at which each method is compared.
![Empirical KDE](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/owt_empirical_entropy.png)
<br />

 We shade along the x-axis to correspend to density of the empirical distribution to provide contextualize single-step results in terms of natural language diversity. 
![FLM With Empirical Entropy](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/owt_fewstep_with_density.png)

While FLM does achieve better distillation results than discrete or hybrid methods, distillation methods in general are still not at the level of natural language, in terms of the extreme edge of the few-step regime (1, 2, and 4 NFE). We observe that most of the mass falls within the range of 5.2-5.7. \
In contrast, the highest entropy achieved by FLM is 5.05, with a perplexity of over 70. 
When using 128 NFE, the baselines (Duo, MDLM, CANDI) are able to achieve an entropy of 5.43 and perplexity below 70. Thus it is possible to achieve similar levels of coherence while being closer to the entropy of natural language. 
<br />
While FLM is significant progress for few-step language generation, the performance level in general is not quite at the level of natural language, at least in terms of entropy and perplexity similarity to real text samples. Closing the gap between few-step generation and natural language entropy remains an open challenge — one where continuous methods appear better positioned due to smoother trajectories, but where current results still fall short. Below we have some expanadable sections that offer an explanation for the performance of distilled FLM. 
<ExpandableSection title="Difference between Teacher Quality and Learnability" client:load>
It is worth briefly discussing why this is the case -- how FLM can result in superior distilled models despite the teacher model underperforming. We provide an informal intuition as to how this is possible. 
<br />
Loosely speaking, the teacher model generates a trajectory using many steps, and the student learns to reach the final destination in a single step. We can view this as having a smaller model (the student) predict the output of a larger model (the teacher), where the teacher is essentially the same model unrolled over time. For simplicity, we can view the teacher as being two of these unrolled steps, and the student as being just one. 

When training the student to match the output of the unrolled model, it is clear that the quality of the unrolled model will have a direct impact on the quality of the distilled model. However, the learnability of the unrolled model also plays a role, as this affects how much of the teacher performance the student is actually able to absorb. For discrete models, we can imagine that in between the two unrolled steps of the teacher model, there is either a discretization step or a stochastic sampling step. This may result in either a certain amount of stochasticity or discontinuity in the target function. As a result, even if the unrolled teacher has very good performance, the actual learnability of this information may suffer due to this intermediate discretization / sampling. 

</ExpandableSection>
<ExpandableSection title="Informal Intuition why Continuous is Good for Distillation" client:load>
FLM operates entirely in the continuous domain. This means that there is no such discontinuity or sampling process involved -- the unrolled teacher model can be viewed as almost a standard neural network, just with twice the number of parameters. This makes the teacher more predictable, which results in the student being able to capture more of the teachers performance. 
<br />

This is our current explanation of why FLM has such great distillation performance -- while FLM does underperform in terms of the base model performance, it's 1024 NFE performance is still quite reasonable. Since the distillation process does not have to deal with discontinuous or stochastic trajectories, the student model is able to capture more of this performance. In contrast, discrete distillation methods must deal with either stochastic or discontinuous trajectories, which creates a bottleneck for the student.
</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Attempts with CANDI Distillation (and why it didn't work as well)" client:load>
We have tried distillation for CANDI using the SDTT (citation here) algorithm, but found it doesn't tend to offer a benefit over the undistilled version. The earlier intuition can offer some form of explanation here: the stochasticity from which position gets discretized interferes with the potential benefits of the continuous trajectories being smooth, preventing CANDI from reaping the benefits of continuous geometry in terms of distillation. 
<br />

Thus we can interpret the outperformance of FLM on distillation as a direct benefit of continuous language models: they have smoother trajectories, and thus student models are able to capture far more information about teacher trajectories than currently possible in discrete distillation. This results in better few step performance. 
</ExpandableSection>


## Conclusion
When working within the scale of GPT-2 models -- which is often a fairly large scale for pretraining from scratch on an academic budget -- generative frontiers based on entropy and generative perplexity are a reasonable way to view the whole scope of model performance. While it is expensive to run, it is still far cheaper than the cost of pretraining itself and it produces conclusions that temperature tuning cannot overcome.
<br /> 

In regards to the papers discussed, we show that FLM represents substantial progress for continuous diffusion models. They currently have an advantage in terms of distillation and few-step performance due to more learnable trajectories. However, the base model comparison demonstrates that discrete and hybrid methods still outperform continuous diffusion models with less NFE. Closing the gap between few-step generation and natural language entropy remains a key challenge.
<br />

It is important to note that the field of continuous diffusion for language is much bigger than just discrete, hybrid, or continuous: there are hybrid diffusion-AR models, latent-diffusion models, hybrid latent-diffusion and AR models -- covering all of them would be an entirely different post. Furthermore, there are many diffusion language model papers that study pretraining and beyond with significantly scaled up models. The goal of this blog was to provide a more accurate view of a tiny slice of where the field stands within the scope of pretraining at the GPT-2 scale.
