## Introduction 
A recent line of work on continuous denoising language models, notably FLM, has reported strong generative performance relative to discrete and hybrid baselines. These results are exciting, as they suggest continuous methods may be closing the gap. However, the comparisons rely on single-point perplexity measurements, which we have previously shown are sensitive to sample entropy — small changes in temperature can invert model rankings entirely.
<br />

In this blog, we apply generative frontier analysis to examine these claims more carefully. We first discuss why OpenWebText is a more meaningful pretraining benchmark than alternatives like LM1B, and why generative perplexity is currently the most practical evaluation metric for comparing models across different paradigms. We then show why single-point perplexity comparisons are unreliable due to entropy sensitivity, and introduce generative frontiers as a tool that addresses this. Using this framework, we make the following observations:
<br />

1. **Model performance is often surprisingly strong early in training** — frontiers at 50k steps closely match those at 1 million steps.
2. Frontier analysis substantially changes the picture for base model comparisons — **properly tuned discrete and hybrid methods outperform continuous denoising at matched entropy**, even with far fewer generation steps.
3. **Continuous denoising does show genuine advantages for few-step generation via distillation**, though current methods still fall short of natural language entropy.

## Pretraining: Why OWT is a Meaningful Dataset 

Recently, there has been renewed interest in using LM1B as a pretraining dataset. To understand why this is problematic, we first discuss why OpenWebText has seemingly become the standard for pretraining data at this scale, and then why LM1B is a less meaningful benchmark based on its design. 

<ExpandableSection title="On OpenWebText" client:load>
It is common to pretrain a ~150 million parameter model with a diffusion transformer architecture on OpenWebText [@Lou_Meng_Ermon_2024] [@Sahoo_Arriola_Schiff_Gokaslan_Marroquin_Chiu_Rush_Kuleshov_2024] [@Shi_Han_Wang_Doucet_Titsias_2024] [@arriola2025block]. Like the original WebText, the data is obtained by scraping all outbound sites on reddit with at least 3 karma, which serves as a data curation mechanism [@radford2021learning]. This results in a large (~9 billion tokens), diverse, yet relatively high quality set of text. For this reason, it has become the de-facto pretraining set for studying language models at this scale: it is usually close to the edge of what is attainable on an academic budget, but is still similar enough to pretraining data to obtain meaningful performance metrics. 
<br />
Additionally, since it is meant as a replication for WebText which GPT-2 was trained on, it is a very well-studied dataset [@radford2021learning] [@Gokaslan2019OpenWeb]. This also enables us to use metrics such as generative perplexity under GPT-2 large as a proxy for data likelihood, which we discuss more in the following section. 
</ExpandableSection>
<ExpandableSection title="On LM1B" client:load>
While there are smaller datasets such as LM1B that can be used for training language models, it is important to note that these datasets often differ from pretraining data in substantial ways. **In particular, LM1B is 1 billion of tokens from news sources shuffled at a sentence level** [@chelba2014billionwordbenchmarkmeasuring].  As a result, there is no notion of coherence across different sentences, since the ordering is fundamentally random. This was intentional -- this benchmark was introduced in 2013, when neural networks were still being compared against n-gram models. Even at the time of introduction, it was primarily intended for likelihood based evaluation -- it was designed to measure whether neural networks could properly model language at the sentence level better than n-gram models. For demonstrating language ability beyond generating random sentences, LM1B is not an ideal dataset for pretraining -- this is why most diffusion language model papers use LM1B for only likelihood or zero shot likelihood evaluation [@Sahoo_Arriola_Schiff_Gokaslan_Marroquin_Chiu_Rush_Kuleshov_2024] [@arriola2025block] [@sahoo2025diffusion] [@Shi_Han_Wang_Doucet_Titsias_2024]. 

</ExpandableSection>
## Generative Perplexity
While the question of what scale to train models at and what data to use is a fairly closed topic, there still remains the evaluation problem. While the original GPT-2 paper demonstrates that pretraining on web-text enabled zero-shot task performance on a wide range of tasks, it is crucial to note that the performance usually required scaling up the number of parameters -- which is difficult when ~150 million is already quite expensive. Furthermore, some of the primary results are zero-shot likelihood across different unseen datasets across different models to demonstrate scaling behavior [@radford2021learning]. This was useful as all models were autoregressive, and thus measured likelihood in the exact same. With different ELBO formulations, comparing the likelihoods directly seems questionable [@theis2016noteevaluationgenerativemodels]. 
<br />

<ExpandableSection title="Why Language Understanding Tasks Aren't Sufficient for Generative Models" client:load>

It is worth mentioning that it is possible to measure model capability in terms of language understanding tasks, similar to those used in the foundational GPT-2 paper. However, it is crucial to note that good performance on these tasks do not necessarily translate into good language generative ability, which is ultimately what we want from generative language models. To see this, we can look at the example of BERT: while the original BERT model had strong performance across natural language understanding and inference [@devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional], it was not suitable on its own for language generation, despite attempts to enable it to serve as a generative model [@wang2019bertmouthspeakbert]. It wasn't until advances in masked diffusion that these models became viable for generative tasks [@Sahoo_Arriola_Schiff_Gokaslan_Marroquin_Chiu_Rush_Kuleshov_2024] [@Shi_Han_Wang_Doucet_Titsias_2024] [@ou2025absorbingdiscretediffusionsecretly]. Strong performance on language understanding tasks is not sufficient evidence of generative quality — which is ultimately what we want to measure for generative language models.

</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Why Log-likelihood Comparisons are Questionable " client:load>
Here we discuss why log-likelihoods may not be a suitable metric when comparing models with different ELBO formulations, and why they do not necessarily reflect generative quality.

### On Comparing Different ELBOs 
It is possible to express the log likelihood as follows, for any variational distribution $q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})$:
$$\log p(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{L}(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}) \,\|\, p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})\big)$$
where $\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}\left[\log \frac{p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})}{q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}\right]$ is the evidence lower bound (ELBO). Since $D_{\mathrm{KL}} \geq 0$, we have $\log p(\mathbf{x}) \geq \mathcal{L}(q)$, with equality if and only if $q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})$. Crucially, the KL divergence term depends on both the true posterior --- which differs across models with different latent variable structures --- and the choice of variational family. As this gap is generally intractable to compute, direct comparison of ELBOs across models with different latent structures is unreliable for model selection [@bishop2006pattern].

### On Likelihoods Themselves
Even if likelihood comparisons between different ELBO formulations were valid, it still may not be relevant to modern language modeling, where the principal concern tends to be generative quality. [@theis2016noteevaluationgenerativemodels] provides an argument for why likelihoods and generative fidelity tend to be independent of each other in high dimensions, and we provide a brief summary here. 
<br />

First, they discuss how to construct a model with excellent generative quality but poor likelihood evaluation through a look-up table with memorized examples from the training set. By definition, this model would place no mass on any other samples besides those that are memorized, which would result in a poor likelihood value for any reasonable validation set. However, by construction, the generative quality would be exactly the same as we would want from the target distribution -- since this model has just memorized exact samples from the training set. 
<br />

Second, they discuss how to construct a model with poor generative quality, but excellent likelihood values. To do this, they start with some model that attains a good likelihood value. They then show that by constructing a mixture between this model and some very poor model that outputs bad quality samples, they obtain a model that has close likelihood values to the original model but will almost always output poor generations. This follows from how likelihoods behave in high dimensions. For a more thorough argument, we highly recommend reading [@theis2016noteevaluationgenerativemodels] -- while it was originally written in the context of image generative models, the core arguments are still relevant to language model evaluation. 
</ExpandableSection>
<ExpandableSection title="Why Generative Perplexity has become standard" client:load>
For this reason, generative perplexity or generative negative log likelihood has gradually become a core tool for comparing different models [@zheng2024masked] [@dieleman2022continuous] [@sahoo2025diffusion] [@sahoo2025esoteric]. All that is required for generative perplexity is the ability to sample from a trained model -- afterwards, we simply run a pre-trained autoregressive model such as GPT-2 Large on the generations and report the likelihoods. We do this for the following reasons:
<br />

1) AR models compute the likelihoods in a single forward pass, and 
2) GPT-2 was trained on web-text, which is the original inspiration for the OpenWebText used widely for pre-training language models. 
</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Generative Perplexity as Data Likelihood Oracle" client:load>

We can view GPT-2 large as an oracle for likelihoods from the target distribution, to the extent that GPT-2 has successfully learned the target distribution. 

While there are other metrics for evaluating generations, generative perplexity seems to be used more frequently because it is easier to interpret. For example, MAUVE is another metric that is used to measure generation quality [@meshchaninov2025compressed]. However, this metric is not as interpretable (it represents a KL divergence based on extracted hidden representations from both reference and generated text), still dependent on having some oracle model, and was originally designed for comparisons for conditional generation [@pillutla2021mauvemeasuringgapneural]. It also requires a fairly large sample size to get meaningful results. Since temperature and NFE can affect sample quality, this can be quite costly. 
<br />

</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Caveats to Generative Perplexity" client:load>
It is important to note that this interpretation depends on the following: 
<br />
1) How well GPT-2 models its training data, WebText
2) How close the training data for the target language model aligns with WebText. 

More concretely, if the target model is trained on a dataset different than WebText or uses a different tokenizer, then perplexity can no longer be reasonably interpreted as a proxy for data likelihood. 

In this case, perplexity would reflect some combination of how close the target model can capture its training data, and how close its training data is to WebText. These two factors may have some interdependent effect on the resulting likelihood from the oracle model, and thus it is more difficult to interpret the resulting perplexity values. In the scenario where the training data differs substantially from WebText, it is probably more suitable to use MAUVE, since it is designed to compare against a reference set of text. 
</ExpandableSection>
## Generative Frontiers: Why They Are Necessary for Accurate Analysis

### Perplexity Sensitivity to Entropy
By definition, perplexity is a measure of how predictable tokens are in a given sequence. Thus if the unigram distribution of a sentence is more degenerate / has mass more concentrated on specific words, it will have a better perplexity value, as the oracle model can simply increase the mass placed on high frequency words throughout its predictions. This does not reflect better language quality necessarily -- it just reflects a more predictable unigram distribution. 
<br />

This has already been observed in [@zheng2024masked], where they demonstrate that even differing logit precisions can result in misleading perplexity values that are revealed via entropy. However, the sensitivity of perplexity of to entropy goes beyond just severe mode collapse -- **even slight changes in entropy can result in more competitive perplexity metrics.** 

<br />
To emphasize that single point comparisons are mutable, we created an interactive app using the frontier data from CANDI that allows the user to alter NFE v.s perplexity comparisons by sliding along the frontiers of different methods. **The differing rankings are not contradictory -- they are merely different slices of the same generative frontier.** 
<br />
<ExpandableSection title="Role of Temperature Tuning in Language modeling" client:load>
Temperature and other inference-time adjustments to logits is already standard practice in autoregressive models, and even larger scale diffusion language models. However, when looking at the literature that studies diffusion LMs at the scale of GPT-2, it is common practice to report a single performance point at either a universal inference setting for all models, or the temperature of 1.0. 

Even if Model A outperforms Model B at temperature 1.0, if Model B can be tuned to beat Model A by lowering the temperature -- which does not require any retraining -- is it really fair to claim Model A is better than Model B? 

</ExpandableSection>

### Interpeting Generative Frontiers Generative Frontiers
For this reason, frontiers of entropy v.s perplexity, as introduced in [@zheng2024masked] and expanded on later in [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], are a very useful tool. These frontiers address the sensitivity of perplexity to entropy by revealing the full frontier, which allows for stronger conclusions. 

<br />
If a model outperforms another at a fixed temperature, this only reflects performance at that temperature and can be reversed via tuning. If a model dominates another in terms of entropy-perplexity frontiers, this means that no amount of tuning (at least for temperatures) can result in better perplexity without sacrificing diversity. **Single-point comparisons without entropy matching compare inference settings; generative frontiers compare model capability.**

<ExpandableSection title="Why Frontiers are not standard practice" client:load>

It is understandable why frontier analysis is not often used — it requires extensive temperature sweeps, and must be repeated for each NFE. Additionally, it results in more nuanced results that may not reveal a clear winner — there may be regions of entropy where one model outperforms  and another where it underperforms. But such complexity isn’t a result of the metric, but rather the task itself — we are trying to evaluate how language-like generations are, and what constitutes good language can vary based on context, reader, etc. The field has shifted towards using perplexity to capture a quality that is very multi-faceted, so it makes sense that resulting comparisons are also multi-faceted.

</ExpandableSection>
### What defines a reasonable entropy

It is natural to wonder what a reasonable entropy is -- if a model outperforms another at a very low entropy, this outperformance may not translate directly into better generations if the entropy is too far from actual language entropy. Thus a reasonable approach is to empirically measure the entropy across the validation set of openwebtext and use it to define intervals across the entropy scale. **We find that most of the mass falls within 5.2-5.7, indicating entropies in this range are close to natural language**. Below we visualize the distribution of entropies, and show how the nfe v.s perplexity changes when we fix an entropy value. This gives a clear picture of how models perform when within a reasonable range of entropy, since this distribution is directly based off of natural language.

## Interesting Side Note: 50k steps is sometimes good enough 
We can use frontier analysis to observe something that may be of interest to those working on pretraining -- often, the generative performance of models very early on in training (50k) steps is quite close to the model's ability after 1,000,000 steps. We visualize this below by pretraining Duo, MDLM, and CANDI for 50k steps and comparing against the frontiers generated by fully trained checkpoints. as one can observe, they are remarkably close. **The generative frontiers of models after 50k steps closely matches the performance at 1 million steps.** Thus when iterating on an algorithm / training framework, it is most likely more than sufficient to use 50k steps -- if a model does not work by this point, it will probably not work by 1,000,000 steps. 
<br />
It is also worth noting that this may indicate a limitation of either generative perplexity or this scale of models in terms of measuring model performance -- it is a commonly known fact that training for longer improves performance on downstream tasks. It is not known whether the above saturation is a result of model capacity or perplexity being unable to capture fine-grained differences in model capability.

## Base Continuous Denoising Models Under Frontier Analysis

Using frontier analysis, we can examine some recent progress with continuous denoising models -- specifically, the flow-matching based framework introduced by [@roos2026categoricalflowmaps] and [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous]. We focus on FLM [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous] as they provide results for pretraining on OWT. CFM [@roos2026categoricalflowmaps] reports text results only on LM1B, which as discussed earlier is less suitable for evaluating generation quality. 
<ExpandableSection title="Experimental Setup" client:load>
We use a ~160 million parameter model trained for 50k steps on OWT for preliminary frontier comparisons when comparing against the continuous baseline from CANDI. When comparing against frontiers for Duo, CANDI, and MDLM, we use the official reported metrics from the FLM paper. These are fair comparisons, as all models use a diffusion transformer architecture, are trained on OpenWebText for up to 1 million steps, and have around the same model size. 
</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Background on FLM" client:load>
In FLM, the authors build on the observation that discrete corruption is ill behaved with respect to continuous noise level or time of the flow matching / diffusion process. They propose to reparameterize time to be linear with respect to the discrete corruption rate. They argue that continuous denoising models make for superior base models than alternative approaches, such as pure discrete or hybrid. They then propose a distillation framework that enables few / single step generation.
</ExpandableSection>

### FLM Is Substantial Progress for Continuous Denoising
The time reparameterization does substantially improve performance of the model in terms of generative. We train an FLM base model for 50k steps and compare it against the pure one-hot diffusion model from CANDI, which is trained on one-hots noised with a VE schedule. This diffusion baseline is also parameterized via softmax on the logits, and trained with cross entropy. we put the results below. 
![FLM v Cont](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/flm_v_cont.png)

<br />
As observed, **FLM is order of magnitude better than the continuous baseline** -- the y axis is on a log scale, so the outperformance is quite dramatic. The original conclusion after the experiment in CANDI was that continuous diffusion is fundamentally broken at high vocabularies. This is clearly not the case -- the reparameterization results in a reasonable base language model. This is clear evidence that the time reparameterization proposed in FLM is unlocking something crucial for continuous language models. 

### Why Frontier Analysis Changes the Picture
The FLM paper reports that the base model achieves a generative perplexity of 62 and entropy of 5.33 at 1024 NFE, outperforming baselines. However, the baselines are measured at operating points with higher entropy. If we take mark the target entropy achieved by FLM at 1024 NFE and compare the perplexities of MDLM, Duo, and CANDI using released results from CANDI, we observe they achieve lower perplexities than 60 by 32 NFE. We annotate the results from CANDI with a vertical red line at entropy 5.33 and show the resulting perplexity for Duo, MDLM, and CANDI. 
![FLM v Baselines](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/frontiers_static.png)
<br /> 
Thus frontier analysis indicates that **properly tuned discrete and hybrid methods can surpass the performance of continuous diffusion models with 32x less steps**, even when aligning the noise schedule with discrete corruption.  We include a visualization demonstrating this below.

## Performance of Distillation Methods under Frontier Analysis

The main contribution of FLM is not only the base model, but the novel distillation method proposed that enables single step / few step generation. We can also apply frontier analysis to better understand these results. We plot the OWT results from their paper on entropy and perplexity frontiers. 
![FLM Few Step](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/flm_distill_fewstep.png)
<br />

It is notable that for few step generation, it appears that the distilled FLM achieves better entropy and perplexity than the distilled duo models. While the distilled versions of MDLM do achieve higher entropy, this does come at the cost of worse perplexity. While we do not have access to the checkpoints for distilled MDLM models, the outperformance in terms of perplexity seem significant enough for the single step regime (~1000) that temperature tuning would not change the relative rankings. **Thus the distilled FLM achieves fairly strong few-step performance relative to discrete baselines.** However, it is not possible to make definitive statements without actual frontiers due to the entropy differences. 
<br /> 

We illustrate the progression in the few step regime of all methods. Notably, discrete methods require more steps to achieve similar few-step performance -- this supports the factorization error argument made in FLM regarding why continuous methods are better suited for distillation. 
![FLM Few-step Gif](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/owt_animated.gif)
### Limitations of Current Few-step Language Generation 
While distilled FLM achieves strong results in the few step regime, it is worth showing the results in the context of the empirical entropy distribution of OWT. 
First, we show a visualization of a KDE estimate of the sample entropy distribution over the OWT evaluation set, which contextualizes the operating points at which each method is compared.
![Empirical KDE](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/owt_empirical_entropy.png)
<br />

We shade along the x-axis to correspond to density of the empirical distribution to provide contextualize single-step results in terms of natural language diversity. 
![FLM With Empirical Entropy](/images/recent_cont_dlm_content/owt_fewstep_with_density.png)

While FLM does achieve better distillation results than discrete or hybrid methods, distillation methods in general are still not at the level of natural language, in terms of the extreme edge of the few-step regime (1, 2, and 4 NFE).
<br />
In contrast, the highest entropy achieved by FLM is 5.05, with a perplexity of over 70. 
When using 128 NFE, the baselines (Duo, MDLM, CANDI) are able to achieve an entropy of 5.43 and perplexity below 70. Thus it is possible to achieve similar levels of coherence while being closer to the entropy of natural language. 
<br />
While FLM is significant progress for few-step language generation, the performance level in general is not quite at the level of natural language, at least in terms of entropy and perplexity similarity to real text samples. Closing the gap between few-step generation and natural language entropy remains an open challenge — one where continuous methods appear better positioned due to smoother trajectories, but where current results still fall short.

## Conclusion
When working within the scale of GPT-2 models -- which is often a fairly large scale for pretraining from scratch on an academic budget -- generative frontiers based on entropy and generative perplexity are a reasonable way to view the whole scope of model performance. While it does require ablating over a wide range of temperatures for each NFE, it is still far cheaper than the cost of pretraining itself and it produces conclusions that temperature tuning cannot invert.
<br /> 

We show that FLM represents substantial progress for continuous diffusion models. They currently have an advantage in terms of distillation and few-step performance. However, the base model comparison demonstrates that discrete and hybrid methods still outperform continuous diffusion models with less NFE. Closing the gap between few-step generation and natural language entropy remains a key challenge.

<ExpandableSection title="Related Papers" client:load>
It is important to note that the field of continuous diffusion for language is much bigger than just discrete, hybrid, or continuous: there are hybrid diffusion-AR models, latent-diffusion models, hybrid latent-diffusion and AR models -- covering all of them would be an entirely different post. Furthermore, there are many diffusion language model papers that study pretraining and beyond with significantly scaled up models. The goal of this blog was to provide a more accurate view of a tiny slice of where the field stands within the scope of pretraining at the GPT-2 scale. Below is a drop down of some interesting papers we think are worth reading related to continuous diffusion / flow matching for text.

- [**CADD**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01329): Proposes hybrid noising kernel on embedding space, shows impressive scaling results on coding tasks. 
- [**CCDD**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03206): Examines theoretical expressivity of different spaces, relates to looped transforms, and motivates hybrid schedules.
- [**LaDir**](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04573): Denoises in latent space to enable reasoning at semantic level instead of token level, then autoregressively decode to text. 
- [**LaDi-RL**](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.01705): Demonstrates that conducting exploration in latent space enables better preservation of diversity among solutions in RL, enabling better performance than RL on text alone.
- [**LADD**](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18114): Explores different approaches of combining latent diffusion with text generation. 
- [**Cosmos**](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.21170): Investigates how to learn smooth latent space for latent diffusion on text, which allows for generation in a compressed space as opposed to directly on the token space.
- [**Duo (Part 2)**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21185) Introduces sampling methods to allow Duo to scale better with inference compute.
