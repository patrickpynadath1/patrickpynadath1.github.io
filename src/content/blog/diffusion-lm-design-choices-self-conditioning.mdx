Discrete versus continuous may be the wrong way to split diffusion language models. We propose **input reliability**, an idea from coding theory: how much the noisy state tells the model about which positions to trust. Masking is an erasure channel, uniform noise is a symmetric channel, and Gaussian noise sits in between. The task decides which one fits. Masking wins on prompted Sudoku, where many clues pin one answer; uniform noise wins on text8, where only whole groups of characters make sense. Self conditioning adds graded reliability, so it helps most where the corruption is the bad match for the task. It also lets continuous models commit tokens, while hybrids add less than their design promises.

</div>

## Introduction
Continuous versus discrete diffusion language models has been a recurring debate, with the former seeing a revival in the past year. The split made sense: continuous recipes used a different set of techniques than discrete methods. But as both lines of research have advanced, the distinction has blurred, as both lines have benefited from techniques originally introduced opposite line. For example, consider the evolution of self conditioning and block diffusion below.
<ExpandableSection title="Self Conditioning: Continuous to Discrete" client:load>
Self Conditioning refers to using the prediction of the denoiser as an input to the next step. It was originally introduced in the context of Gaussian binary diffusion by [@chen2022analog], and became common in embedding diffusion approaches [@strudelSelfconditionedEmbeddingDiffusion2022] [@dieleman2022continuous], latent diffusion approaches [@shabalin2025tencdm]. However, it has recently been demonstrating benefits for pure discrete methods: [@joLoopholingDiscreteDiffusion2026] demonstrates that loopholing, a closely related technique, benefits discrete methods. DiffusionGemma further demonstrates the effectiveness of self conditioning towards uniform diffusion [@diffusiongemma2026].
</ExpandableSection>

<ExpandableSection title="Block Diffusion: Discrete to Continuous" client:load>
Block diffusion was popularized for masked diffusion in [@arriola2025block] as a way to interpolate between autoregressive generation and masked diffusion. By providing clean token context for the denoising block, it improves masked diffusion performance in terms of generation quality. The same core principal -- committing fully denoised tokens to clean context to enable anchoring for denoising future blocks -- have been shown to benefit continuous diffusion methods [@yang2026sigma] [@schiff2026clock] [@chavan2026lafa].
</ExpandableSection>

If recipes in both discrete and continuous diffusion make discrete commitments and operate on soft inputs, then the distinction between discrete and continuous diffusion may not decide what techniques are applicable to different recipes. In this post, we borrow from classical coding theory and propose categorizing methods by the **reliability of the input**, or how much the noisy state tells the model about which positions to trust. Under this axis, masked diffusion can be linked to an erasure channel that gives perfect reliability information for each position, and uniform can be linked to a symmetric channel that gives the same reliability value across all positions being decoded. Continuous methods fall in between the two extremes, as they provide a range of reliability values across different positions. 

This categorization gives insight into when a specific corruption form may prove beneficial: erasure suits redundant structure, where several clues pin each position; and symmetric channel suits synergistic strucutre, where the answer depends on the correct combination of multiple choices.  Furthermore, this provides an alternative perspective on when self conditioning helps: it adds graded reliability to any recipe, which moves each one toward the middle of the spectrum and helps most where the corruption was a poor match for the task. 
We test this hypothesis by ablating self conditioning and hybrid corruption across various recipes for 3 distinct tasks, and we tune a range of samplers for each method to ensure we capture the full potential of each recipe. We discuss how the experimental results agree with our axis, and also note specific observations that do not fully align. 
<div className="spark">

- Discrete vs continuous is blurring: both camps now commit discrete tokens and rely on soft inputs.
- We propose a different axis from coding theory, input reliability: masked is an erasure channel, uniform a symmetric channel, Gaussian in between.
- Erasure suits redundant tasks and the symmetric channel synergistic ones; self conditioning adds graded reliability and helps the mismatched family most.
- We test this by ablating self conditioning and hybrids across 21 recipes on three tasks, tuning the sampler for every model.

</div>

## Background

<div className="spark">

- Self conditioning: the model's previous prediction, fed back as an input.
- Coding theory: masked diffusion is an erasure channel, uniform diffusion a symmetric channel, and Gaussian diffusion gives graded reliability in between.
- Redundant structure favors the erasure channel and synergistic structure the symmetric channel.

</div>

This section covers the three ideas the rest of the post builds on: self conditioning, the channels of coding theory, and the difference between redundant and synergistic structure.

### Self Conditioning

<div className="spark">

- Self conditioning feeds the model's previous prediction back as an extra input.
- It started in Gaussian diffusion, is standard there, and also helps simplex, spherical, uniform and masked diffusion.
- Existing explanations mostly target continuous diffusion; why it helps discrete diffusion is still open.

</div>

Self conditioning feeds the denoiser's previous prediction back as an extra input to the next step; in our models, this is the expected embedding under the previous prediction, and zero at the first step. It was introduced for Gaussian diffusion on binary bits [@chen2022analog] and is now a standard part of continuous diffusion language models [@dieleman2022continuous] [@strudel2022self] [@gulrajani2023likelihood] [@huELFEmbeddedLanguage2026] [@chen2026langflow] [@yang2026continuousdiffusionscalescompetitively] [@chavan2026lafa] [@yang2026sigma] [@shabalin2025tencdm] [@meshchaninov2026cosmoscompressedsmoothlatent] [@meshchaninov2026train]. It also helps simplex, spherical, uniform and masked diffusion [@deschenaux2026simplex] [@chemseddine2026spherical] [@joLoopholingDiscreteDiffusion2026] [@diffusiongemma2026], and can even serve as a generative method on its own [@deschenaux2026loopholingwaylanguagemodeling].

Why it helps is still open. Existing explanations mostly target continuous diffusion: pseudo-conditioning against the long tails of Gaussian noise in high dimensions [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], correcting a mismatch between training and inference [@davisDesignSpaceContinuous2026], and a link to fixed-point solvers in flow models [@yooSelfconditionedFlowMap2026a]. They do not explain why it also helps discrete diffusion, or why it still helps a multi-layer recipe that already anchors discrete tokens [@davisDesignSpaceContinuous2026].

### Ideas From Coding Theory

<div className="spark">

- Masked: an erasure channel. Exact reliability: a position is revealed and correct, or masked and empty.
- Uniform: a symmetric channel. The same reliability everywhere: a swapped token looks like any other.
- Gaussian: in between. Each position gets its own graded reliability.

</div>

Coding theory studies how a decoder recovers a message sent through a noisy channel, and it tells channels apart by what each received symbol says about its own reliability. Each corruption family in diffusion language models matches one classical channel.

- **Masked diffusion is an erasure channel.** A position is either revealed and correct, or masked and empty, and the model can see which. Reliability is exact.
- **Uniform diffusion is a symmetric channel.** A corrupted token is replaced by another valid token, so every position looks the same whether or not it was swapped. Reliability is the same everywhere, and only context can find the swapped tokens. The binary version is the binary symmetric channel.
- **Gaussian diffusion sits in between.** Noise is added to each position's embedding, and each position gets its own graded reliability, set by how much closer the noisy point is to the nearest token than to the runner-up.

Simplex diffusion slides between the last two: at a small Dirichlet concentration it reduces to uniform diffusion, and at a large one it follows a path with Gaussian fluctuations [@deschenaux2026simplex]. Our simplex models use a small concentration, so they sit near the uniform end. Whether the state commits hard tokens or carries soft values is a second axis ([Figure L](#figure-l)). In coding terms this is hard versus soft messages: a hard message passes on a decision, one token, and drops how sure it was; a soft message passes on the graded belief. Masked and uniform states hold hard tokens, a continuous state holds soft values, and self conditioning adds a soft message to any of them, which is why it matters when a continuous model is made to commit tokens ([below](#self-conditioning-lets-continuous-models-commit-tokens)).

<div id="figure-l"></div>

<ReliabilityMap client:visible kicker="Figure L · two axes" />

<ExpandableSection title="Reliability as a log-likelihood ratio" client:load>

For one position, let $L$ be the log-likelihood ratio between two candidate clean tokens given the noisy state. Its size $|L|$ is the reliability.

- **Erasure.** A revealed position has $|L| = \infty$ and a masked one has $L = 0$: two spikes, and the model sees which.
- **Symmetric.** If a token is replaced with probability $\beta$ by one drawn uniformly from a vocabulary of size $V$, then for an observed token $y$ the ratio between "the clean token is $y$" and "it is some other $z$" is $\log \frac{1 - \beta + \beta / V}{\beta / V}$, the same at every position: one spike. For a binary symmetric channel with flip probability $p$, $|L| = \log\frac{1-p}{p}$ everywhere.
- **Gaussian.** For one bit sent as $\pm 1$ with noise level $\sigma$, $L = 2Y/\sigma^2$, which given $X = +1$ is normal with mean $2/\sigma^2$ and variance $4/\sigma^2$ [@richardson2008modern] (§4.1.5): a whole spread of reliabilities. For tokens, each comparison against a competitor behaves like this one-bit case, so the spread is widest against close competitors.

</ExpandableSection>

<div className="breakout-body">

**A sample as stacks of bits.** A sample needs a certain number of bits on average: the answers to a series of yes-or-no questions that, taken together, pin it down. Generating a sample means settling all of them. Draw each position as a stack of boxes, one box per bit; some stacks are taller because some tokens are harder to predict. Boxes of the same color carry the same information: once one is known, the others follow. Counting every box gives the bits each position needs on its own; counting each color once gives the bits the whole sequence needs. The difference is the information the positions share, the total correlation [@watanabe1960information].

<BoxSetup client:visible />

**Masked diffusion as peeling.** Under masked diffusion, a position is either masked, so none of its boxes are known, or revealed, so all of them are. Revealing a box fixes every box of the same color elsewhere, so the sequence pays for a shared bit only once. In coding terms, revealing is the erasure channel, and turning the matching boxes known is one round of the peeling decoder [@richardson2008modern] (§3.19): whenever a clue has exactly one unknown bit left, that bit becomes known, which can unlock another clue.

<BoxDiscount client:visible />

**Revealing several positions at once.** Autoregressive generation reveals one position per step, after seeing all the earlier ones, so every shared bit is paid for once. Masked diffusion reveals several per step, and positions revealed together cannot use each other: if two share a color, each is drawn as if the other were unknown, and the two draws can disagree. This is the factorization error of parallel decoding: the step samples each position from its own marginal instead of from their joint, and the cost grows with the total correlation among the positions revealed together [@li2025convergence]. A confidence-ordered sampler reduces the damage by revealing first the positions whose bits are already fixed.

<BoxThreeWays client:visible />

**Each corruption hands over bits differently.** **Masking** works one position at a time: a position's boxes are either all hidden or all shown, and the model knows which, so its reliability is exact. **Uniform noise** gives a little about every position at once: each shown token is probably right, but every position has the same single level of reliability, and nothing marks which tokens were swapped. **Gaussian noise** also touches every position, but by different amounts. It adds noise to each token's embedding, so the embedding table acts like a constellation of points [@richardson2008modern] (§5.4). Bits that separate far-apart groups of tokens are easy to read, bits that separate close neighbors are hard, and each position's reliability depends on how close its noisy point landed to the nearest token compared with the runner-up. The figure shows this for four tokens and two bits.

The sampler turns the prediction into tokens or noise for the next step and throws away the model's graded belief about the rest, the sampling wall of Jo et al. ### Redundant vs Synergistic Structure

<div className="spark">

- Redundant (copy-like): several clues each pin the same answer. Synergistic (parity-like): the answer lies only in a combination.
- At equal capacity, erasure is best and the symmetric channel worst for redundant structure; for synergistic structure, the order flips. Gaussian is always in between.

</div>

Tasks differ in how the answer depends on the positions around it. In **redundant** (copy-like) structure, several clues each pin the same answer, so any one of them is enough. In **synergistic** (parity-like) structure, the answer lies only in a combination: no single clue says much, and the clues together say everything. These are the two poles of O-information [@rosas2019quantifying]; a copy is the purest redundancy and a parity check the purest synergy.

Coding theory tells us which channel suits each. At equal capacity, the erasure channel gives the most information about a value seen several times and the symmetric channel the least; for a parity of several values, the order flips, and the Gaussian channel always sits in between. This is the result known as the extremes of information combining [@richardson2008modern] (§4.10.2). The intuition is that erasure is all or nothing. For a copy, one revealed look is enough. For a parity, every other position must be revealed, which rarely happens, while a symmetric channel leaves every position slightly unsure but the combination still informative.

We place each of our tasks on this axis in [Tasks](#tasks).

### How We Study the Axes

<div className="spark">

- Self conditioning moves recipes along the reliability axis: it softens masked diffusion's exact reliability and gives uniform diffusion the reliability it lacks.
- Hybrids move recipes along the commitment axis: they pair a discrete view that commits tokens with a continuous view that refines soft values.
- Without self conditioning, the task structure should decide between masked and uniform diffusion; with it, the mismatched family should gain most.

</div>

We move recipes along the two axes of [Figure L](#figure-l) with two ablations.

Self conditioning crosses the reliability axis. It feeds back the model's belief about every position, which adds graded reliability to any recipe. It softens masked diffusion's exact reliability, gives uniform diffusion the per-position reliability it lacks, and makes Gaussian diffusion's graded reliability follow the model's belief instead of only the noise. Without it, each sampling step turns the prediction into hard tokens or fresh noise and throws the rest of the belief away, what Jo et al. call the sampling wall [@joLoopholingDiscreteDiffusion2026]; self conditioning carries the belief across it.

Hybrids cross the commitment axis. A hybrid corrupts the same tokens two ways at once, pairing a discrete view that commits hard tokens with a continuous view that refines soft values. Its continuous view adds graded evidence too, but that evidence comes from the noise, and the schedule sets how much there is. Self conditioning passes the model's own belief, which sharpens as sampling goes on, and adds it to any recipe without changing its corruption.

Combined with the structure of each task ([Tasks](#tasks)), this predicts:

| | Sudoku (redundant) | text8 (synergistic) |
|---|---|---|
| Which family wins without SC | Masked: erasure is best for redundancy | Uniform: the symmetric channel is best for synergy |
| SC on masked | Little to gain; graded beliefs can blur its exact reliability | Room to gain: erasure is the worst channel here |
| SC on uniform | Room to gain: the symmetric channel is the worst here | Little to gain: already the best channel |
| SC on Gaussian | Gains: its margins now follow the model's belief, not only the noise | Gains, for the same reason |

### Methods Studied 

<div className="spark">

- Continuous: Gaussian embedding diffusion, spherical interpolants (two variants) and simplex diffusion.
- Discrete: masked diffusion and uniform diffusion, with uniform in the posterior parameterization.
- Hybrids: one discrete view paired with one continuous view.
- Continuous and hybrid models use the CDCD adaptive schedule; discrete models use uniform time sampling.

</div>

We break down the general goal of understanding self conditioning into more digestible interaction studies -- we hold one family of methods fixed and ablate self conditioning to investigate performance differences. For continuous methods, we include the following corruption approaches: 
1. Gaussian embedding diffusion, essentially the recipe used in [@dieleman2022continuous]
2. Spherical interpolants, both the variant proposed in [@deschenaux2026language] and [@chemseddine2026spherical]
3. Simplex diffusion models as used in [@deschenaux2026simplex]

For discrete diffusion, we focus on masked diffusion [@sahooSimpleEffectiveMasked2024] and uniform diffusion. For uniform, we choose to work with a denoiser parameterization, as this allows us to fix the model parameterization across all methods [@gourevitch2026uniform]. 

For hybrid methods, we investigate the product space produced by the discrete methods and continuous methods. While our codebase does allow for combining different continuous views, we focus on discrete and continuous combinations as part of our goal is to understand how self conditioning affects performance if discrete anchoring is already provided to a continuous method. 

Continuous and hybrid models use the CDCD adaptive noise schedule [@dieleman2022continuous]; discrete models use uniform time sampling.

### Tasks

<div className="spark">

- Prompted Sudoku is redundant: one answer per prompt, and each cell is pinned by its row, column and box.
- text8 is synergistic: no single character is wrong by itself, and a word is valid only as a whole.
- TinyStories falls in between, and its large vocabulary lets context spot uniform swaps.
- Larger tasks such as OpenWebText or TinyGSM were not practical at this breadth.

</div>

We study three tasks, chosen to sit at different points of the redundant–synergistic axis.

- **Sudoku, in its prompted form, is redundant.** The prompt determines the answer, and each answer cell is pinned by its row, its column and its box at once: three overlapping clues for one value.
- **text8 is synergistic.** No single character is wrong by itself. A word is valid only if all its letters make sense together, and many different words are possible. The metric rewards exactly this: it counts valid words of five or more letters, an all-or-nothing check over a group. Text also has some redundant pieces ("q" is almost always followed by "u"), but the structure the task rewards is mainly synergistic.
- **TinyStories** is a prompted task with many valid continuations, so it falls in between.

<ExpandableSection title="More detail: why prompted Sudoku is redundant" client:load>

Sudoku's rules look synergistic: in a row, no single digit says much about another, and only the nine together must be all different. What matters for the channel view, though, is the evidence that passes *through the channel*, and in our task the prompt shapes it.

- **The givens are never corrupted.** Only the answer cells are noised. The rules tie the answer cells to the givens as much as to each other, and the givens always arrive clean, so the joint structure is already settled by the prompt.
- **Given the prompt, the answer is fixed.** Every answer cell is a function of the same prompt, so every answer cell the channel reveals points to the same single solution. A revealed cell never argues for a second valid answer.
- **Each revealed cell helps on its own.** Take one blank cell. The givens in its row, column and box have already ruled out most digits. Each other answer cell in those units that the channel reveals can rule out one more, whether or not its neighbors are revealed, and the cell can often be pinned by its row, its column or its box separately. The evidence adds up piece by piece, from several directions at once. In a pure parity check, by contrast, knowing all but one of the other positions tells you nothing; the answer appears only when the last one arrives.

That is the copy-like signature: several partial clues about one fixed value, each useful alone. It also shows why the symmetric channel does badly here. An erased neighbor says nothing, which is harmless. A swapped neighbor shows a wrong digit, and if that digit happens to be the blank cell's true value, it rules out the right answer. The erasure channel never lies, so every clue it passes can be trusted as it arrives.

All of this depends on the prompt. A model that generates complete grids from scratch faces the rules alone: no answer is fixed, and the all-different constraints tie the generated digits together synergistically. The channel view then predicts the opposite ordering, with uniform diffusion closing the gap to masked diffusion or overtaking it. We test this by [removing Sudoku hints](#removing-sudoku-hints-from-redundant-to-synergistic).

</ExpandableSection>

#### Context Can Recover Reliability

Our axis is input-level: what the noisy token itself says. A model also reads context, and a swap it can spot works like an erasure. With a large vocabulary and skewed token frequencies, a uniform swap almost always lands on a rare token that does not fit, so uniform diffusion behaves more like masked diffusion and the symmetric channel's edge on synergistic structure fades. This fits TinyStories, with 10,000 BPE tokens, where masked and uniform diffusion tie and self conditioning adds little to uniform diffusion. With 27 characters, text8 keeps swaps hard to spot, and uniform diffusion leads.

These tasks are not ideal: the more informative battery of experiments would be to use pretraining on OpenWebText to understand convergence and generative quality; and TinyGSM as used in [@liu2023tinygsmachieving80gsm8k] [@deschenaux2026language]. However, given the sheer breadth of the design space we want to investigate for this study, it is simply not practical to use these tasks.

<ExpandableSection title="More detail: each task and its metric" client:load>

**Sudoku**

Sudoku is a classic reasoning task used in a variety of recent papers [@deschenaux2026language; @kim2025train; @agarwal2026posterior; @tang2026dbtm]. Additionally, it is the easiest to evaluate as we can directly read accuracy metrics. However, it is further from how actual language is structured for the following reasons: 
1. Given a prompt, the distribution is collapsed to a single point. Real text allows for multiple correct ways of responding to a prompt. 
2. The tokens are essentially uniform in terms of frequency. Real language is extremely asymmetric. 

For this reason, we need additional tasks to be able to properly gauge each method's performance under ablations.

**Text8**

We choose text8 as it represents a far less collapsed task: we can measure the number of unique and valid words with length greater than $n$ characters. For our experiments, we choose $5$. This is a useful task as it reflects whether methods are able to coordinate individual tokens together into longer spans of coherent text; and it also is relatively easy to measure. 

However, it is still quite distant from real language, as we work in the character space as opposed to the token space. As discussed in works such as [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion] and [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous], vocabulary size has a direct impact on the noise schedule, which could affect performance.

**TinyStories**

The most realistic task we look at is TinyStories, where we train a model to generate meaningful continuations of some story. This is the closest, as it uses a somewhat realistic vocabulary size (10k BPE) and is actual language generation. However, this also makes it the hardest to evaluate. It would not be appropriate to use generative perplexity and entropy, as these measure KL to the distribution encoded by the AR model. Furthermore, we would ideally like to take advantage of the ground truth continuations we have. We can choose SOL from [@sol2026], but this would pose significant difficulties for tuning, as our tuning stage of the pipeline is quite involved. BLEU would reward repeating generic phrases, which could result in overly generic stories. We choose to proceed with MAUVE from [@pillutla2021mauvemeasuringgapneural], using an AR model trained on TinyStories as the feature extractor. 

</ExpandableSection>

### Training Protocol 

<div className="spark">

- Every recipe uses the same model and training: an 8-block DiT of width 512, 20,000 steps, batch 256, AdamW at 3e-4, EMA weights.
- Self conditioning: a no-gradient pass on 90% of each batch feeds back the expected embedding; at sampling time it adds no network calls.
- One training seed per model. Shared settings are a practical choice, not a principled one.

</div>

Every recipe uses the same model and training: an 8-block diffusion transformer of width 512, trained for 20,000 steps, with one training seed per model.

<ExpandableSection title="More detail: training settings" client:load>

- **Architecture.** A diffusion transformer (DiT) with 8 blocks, hidden size 512, 8 attention heads and dropout 0.1. Methods that diffuse in an embedding space (Gaussian, spherical, vMF) use 512-dimensional token embeddings.
- **Optimizer.** AdamW, learning rate 3e-4 with 2,500 linear warmup steps and no decay afterwards; betas (0.9, 0.999), no weight decay, gradient clipping at 1.0.
- **Length of training.** 20,000 optimizer steps at a global batch of 256 sequences (4 gradient-accumulation steps), bf16 mixed precision, on a single GPU.
- **EMA.** We keep an exponential moving average of the weights with decay 0.9999 and evaluate the EMA weights.
- **Sequence lengths.** Sudoku 180 tokens (puzzle prompt plus solution), text8 256 characters, TinyStories 512 BPE tokens (10k vocabulary).
- **Self conditioning.** On 90% of each training batch, the model first runs a no-gradient forward pass, and the expected embedding under that prediction is fed back as an extra input. At sampling time the previous step's prediction is fed back, so self conditioning adds no network calls.
- **Seeds.** One training seed per model. The noise this leaves is about 3 points on Sudoku, 0.5 words on text8 and 0.045 MAUVE on TinyStories.

The choice of using the same training settings for all methods is not based on principle, but practicality -- the ideal case would be to take each recipe and search for the best architectural choices, learning rate schedule, and optimizer as there is no guarantee that different generative methods have identical optimal settings. However, we choose not to do this as 1) these experiments are at such a small scale that the settings would not transfer to any practical scale; and 2) even without training sweeps, the number of runs and compute needed is large, for an academic scale. 

</ExpandableSection>

<div className="callout">
<span className="callout-label">Protocol (reference)</span>

- **Models.** 8-layer diffusion transformer, width 512, 8 heads; 16 layers for the depth arm. 20,000 steps, batch 256, learning rate 3e-4 after 2,500 warmup steps, no decay. One training seed per model. Embedding methods (Gaussian, spherical, vMF) use 512 dimensions.
- **SC.** The model receives the expected embedding under its previous prediction. Training: an extra no-grad forward pass on 90% of each batch. Sampling: the previous step's prediction, so no extra network calls.
- **Tasks and metrics.** Sudoku, hard split (exact-solve accuracy, higher is better; 976 test puzzles). text8 (mean distinct valid words of 5+ letters per sample, higher is better; held-out real text scores 16.53; 2,048 test samples). TinyStories, 10k BPE (validation MAUVE on TinyStories-33M features, higher is better; test MAUVE plus SOL and a judge, see below; 2,048 test prompts).
- **Samplers.** Eight for every model: native, native greedy, renoise, renoise greedy, EB greedy, EB churn, and Ψ on two clocks (Ψ·c: the corruption level; Ψ·t: the controller time). Ψ·t was tuned in a separate run on the same checkpoints, prompts and seeds. Two more for the 24 pure continuous models: anchor native and anchor renoise (separate run, same checkpoints, prompts and seeds).
- **Tuning and selection.** Optuna on validation only (GP for native, renoise and EB; multivariate TPE for Ψ and anchoring; both values for the two-option samplers), 2 to 24 trials per sampler. Stage 1: 64 prompts, two seeds. Stage 2: each sampler's top 3 on 256 fresh prompts, fresh seed, bootstrap interval. The stage-2 winner is tested once at NFE 128 with two seeds. NFE counts every network call.
- **NFE 512 check.** Each sampler's winning settings at 512 calls on a fixed 128 test prompts, one seed. Reported only; no selection reads it.
- **Noise for one seed.** The two test seeds differ by up to 1.7 points (Sudoku) and 0.13 words (text8); stage-2 intervals are ±2 to ±6 points (Sudoku), ±0.3 to ±0.5 words (text8). Working thresholds: about 3 points (Sudoku), 0.5 words (text8). TinyStories: about 0.045 MAUVE, the half-width of a paired bootstrap 95% interval over test prompts for a tuned-minus-untuned gap; MAUVE's k-means noise alone is only 0.018.
- **Exact settings.** sddc `configs/sweep/{rerun_protocol,rerun_common}.yaml`, `configs/inference/inference_policies/{hybrid_renoising,eb_churn,psi,anchoring}.yaml`, `experiments/rerun/evaluation/*.json`.

### Sampler Pipeline

<div className="spark">

- Every model is tuned over the same eight samplers at 128 network calls; pure continuous models also get two random-anchoring samplers.
- Optuna searches on validation, each sampler's top 3 are re-scored on fresh prompts, and only the winner is tested once.
- Commit: freeze a position to one token. EB: commit the surest positions. Random anchoring: commit at random on a schedule. Ψ: mix native and renoise steps per position.
- Selection uses the task metric: Sudoku accuracy, text8 valid words, TinyStories MAUVE.
- One set of samplers runs on every recipe whose model predicts a clean-token posterior and whose process has both a forward and a reverse step (see the breakout).

</div>

We assess recipes by the final samples produced, making our results heavily contingent on the sampler performance for each method. To make sure we are comparing methods at their best performance, we rely heavily on automated sampler tuning for each recipe. We outline the sampler tuning pipeline below.

<TuningProcess client:visible />

<ExpandableSection title="More detail: the tuning protocol, step by step" client:load>

1. **Fixed budget.** Every sampler is run at 128 network calls (NFE), counting every call, including the final decoding call.
2. **Samplers.** Each model is tuned with eight samplers: native, native greedy, renoise, renoise greedy, EB greedy, EB churn, and Ψ on two clocks. The pure continuous models also get two random-anchoring samplers (anchor native, anchor renoise). The breakout below explains what each one changes, and how one set of samplers runs on every recipe.
3. **Search spaces.** Each sampler exposes a small set of knobs. All of them tune the prediction temperature (0.5 to 1.5). EB samplers also tune the entropy bound (0.02 to 0.5 nats). EB churn also tunes how often committed positions are re-noised and when that stops. Ψ tunes its schedule, step size and top-p. Anchoring tunes the commitment power (0.33 to 3) and whether committed tokens are sampled or taken greedily. The two "greedy" variants have only two settings, so both are simply evaluated.
4. **Search.** Optuna proposes settings: Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization for native, renoise and the EB samplers, and multivariate TPE for Ψ and anchoring, which have conditional or categorical knobs. Each search starts from the sampler's default settings and runs 2 to 24 trials depending on the size of the space.
5. **Stage 1 (screening).** Every trial is scored on 64 validation prompts with two sampling seeds.
6. **Stage 2 (confirmation).** Each sampler's top 3 settings are re-scored on 256 fresh validation prompts with a fresh seed, with a bootstrap interval. The best stage-2 result across all samplers is the model's tuned sampler. Re-scoring on fresh prompts guards against picking a setting that only got lucky on the stage-1 prompts.
7. **Test.** Only the winner is scored on the test split, once, at 128 NFE with two seeds. Untuned results use each model's native sampler at temperature 1 with the same budget.

<AnchorSchedule client:visible />

</ExpandableSection>

Three terms are used throughout the results. A sampler **commits** a position when it sets that position to one token and freezes it for the rest of sampling. **Entropy-bounded (EB)** samplers commit the positions the model is surest about [@benhamu2025accelerated]: at each step they take positions from most to least certain, for as long as the summed entropy of the chosen positions, not counting the largest one, stays under a tuned bound. **Random anchoring** ignores the model's confidence and commits positions at random on a schedule, which mimics the order a masked process imposes. Finally, **Ψ** mixes the native and renoise samplers [@deschenaux2026psi]: at each step, every position takes the native step with some probability and is re-noised from the model's prediction otherwise.

The metric that drives selection is the task metric: exact-solve accuracy for Sudoku, distinct valid words of five or more letters for text8, and MAUVE for TinyStories. The full sampler-tuning protocol is summarized in [Figure T](#sampler-pipeline).

### What a sampler is allowed to ask for

A recipe in our codebase is built from **views**, one per corruption; a hybrid has two. Each view bundles a representation (tokens to states), a schedule (time to noise coefficients) and a process (the corruption and its reverse). The network makes a single prediction that all views share. A sampler touches a recipe only through this short list:

| The sampler needs | Supplied by |
|---|---|
| A clean-token posterior $p_\theta(x_0 \mid z_t)$ at every position | the model, in the posterior parameterization |
| A starting state at $t = 1$ | the process's prior |
| The native reverse step $z_t \to z_s$, given that posterior | the process |
| The forward kernel $q(z_s \mid x_0)$, to re-noise a clean guess | the process |
| The clean state of a token, to commit a position | the representation |
| Final tokens | one terminal prediction, decoded greedily or by sampling |

So **any recipe whose model predicts a clean-token posterior, and whose process can write both its forward kernel and its native reverse step, can run every sampler below.** That covers masked, uniform, Gaussian, spherical, simplex and the hybrids. The exceptions are recipes whose network does not output a posterior: SEDD predicts score ratios and keeps its own analytic sampler. A plug-in estimate, as in DUO, can be converted to a posterior; we train uniform diffusion with the posterior parameterization directly, so every model in this post predicts the same kind of object. The codebase checks each pairing before sampling and refuses an incompatible one with a reason, and a generated table records every family-by-sampler combination.

### One loop with five choices

Every sampler below runs the same loop. Each step makes one network call, then fills five slots:

1. **Clean estimate.** Keep the full posterior, sample one token per position from it, or take its most likely token.
2. **Positions.** Update all positions, or choose some: at random, by entropy, or on a fixed schedule.
3. **Transition.** Move the state with the process's native reverse step, or discard it and re-noise the clean estimate with the forward kernel.
4. **Commitment.** None, freeze (a committed position is set to its token's clean state and stays there), or revise (committed positions can be released again).
5. **Terminal decoding.** Greedy or sampled, chosen separately from the slots above.

Every run uses 127 updates plus one terminal call, so 128 network calls in all. Temperature and top-p belong to the model's prediction, not to the sampler, so every sampler can tune them.

### The sampler classes

**Native (and native greedy).** Each process's own reverse step, at every position. In masked diffusion, a masked position is revealed with probability $(\alpha_s - \alpha_t)/(1 - \alpha_t)$ and its token is drawn from the posterior; revealed positions stay. In uniform diffusion, the step mixes the exact bridges $q(z_s \mid z_t, x_0)$ over the posterior. In Gaussian diffusion, it is a stochastic step from the reverse posterior around the expected clean embedding. Simplex and spherical diffusion use their own bridge and tangent-flow steps. *Native greedy* feeds the reverse step a one-hot argmax instead of the full posterior. Slots: posterior, all positions, native step, no commitment.

**Renoise (and renoise greedy).** Sample a clean sequence $\hat x_0$ from the posterior, then draw the next state fresh from the forward kernel, $z_s \sim q(z_s \mid \hat x_0)$. The current state is discarded. This needs only a posterior and a forward kernel, so it runs on every process, and it can re-mask a revealed token or move a settled point. *Renoise greedy* takes $\hat x_0$ as the argmax. Slots: sampled (or argmax) tokens, all positions, forward kernel, no commitment.

**Ψ.** A mixture of the two above, drawn per position [@deschenaux2026psi]: each position takes the native step with probability $\kappa_t$ and is re-noised otherwise. $\kappa_t = 1$ is native and $\kappa_t = 0$ is renoise, token for token. The schedule for $\kappa_t$ comes from ReMDM's remasking scale [@wang2025remasking]. Ψ was introduced for discrete diffusion; we extend it to continuous processes by re-noising with each process's own forward kernel. Tuned: the schedule shape, its strength, top-p and temperature.

**Entropy-bounded: EB greedy and EB churn.** At each step, every uncommitted position is scored by the entropy of its predicted distribution. Positions are taken from most to least certain while the summed entropy, not counting the largest, stays under a tuned bound $\gamma$ [@benhamu2025accelerated]. Each chosen position is committed to its most likely token and frozen; the rest take the native step. In a masked model, committing is revealing. In a continuous model, committing sets the position's state to its token's clean embedding and holds it there, so even a Gaussian model can decode in order of confidence. *EB churn* also releases a few committed positions at random each step and re-noises them with the forward kernel, at a tuned rate that drops to zero partway through sampling. Slots: argmax tokens, entropy-chosen positions, native step for the rest, freeze (churn: revise).

**Random anchoring (continuous models only).** Commitment without the model's confidence. After step $k$ of $S$, a share $(k/S)^p$ of positions is committed, chosen uniformly at random; the last step commits the rest. Each new position gets a token sampled from (or the argmax of) the prediction at that step. The other positions take the native step (*anchor native*) or are re-noised (*anchor renoise*). This mimics the reveal schedule of a masked process on a continuous model. Slots: sampled or argmax tokens, scheduled random positions, native step or forward kernel, freeze.

### What this does and does not claim

Running every sampler on every recipe makes the comparisons fair: each recipe gets the same chances to find a good sampler. It does not make every pairing principled. Renoising a masked model re-masks tokens that the masked reverse process would never re-mask, and entropy-bounded commitment freezes positions that a Gaussian process never freezes. These are valid ways to produce a sample, not the exact reverse of the process the model was trained on. That is why we tune and select on validation rather than assume one sampler is right for each recipe.

### Task Structure Decides Between Masked and Uniform

If input reliability is the right axis, the structure of the task, not the split between discrete and continuous, should decide which of masked and uniform diffusion does better.

#### Without Self Conditioning, the Task Decides Between Masked and Uniform

- Without self conditioning, masked diffusion wins on Sudoku and uniform diffusion wins on text8.
- On TinyStories they tie, as expected when context lets the model spot uniform swaps.

Under the usual split, masked and uniform diffusion are siblings. Without self conditioning, they behave like opposites, in the direction the channel view predicts. On Sudoku, whose structure is redundant, masked diffusion is clearly ahead. On text8, whose structure is synergistic, uniform diffusion is ahead. On TinyStories the two tie, which fits a large vocabulary in which context can spot uniform swaps ([Tasks](#tasks)).

#### Removing Sudoku Hints: From Redundant to Synergistic

<div className="spark">

- Removing hints breaks the chain of deductions that makes prompted Sudoku redundant.
- Without self conditioning, masked diffusion's lead disappears, and uniform diffusion finds more distinct valid solutions.
- With self conditioning, almost every model fills an empty board, though its valid samples repeat more when a puzzle has many solutions.

</div>

Prompted Sudoku is redundant because the prompt fixes the answer. With all its hints, a puzzle is a chain of deductions in which each filled cell pins others, the peeling decoder from the bit view. Removing hints breaks the chain: a cell's value is then fixed only jointly with many others, and with no hints left the model faces the rules alone, which are synergistic ([Tasks](#tasks)). The channel view predicts that uniform diffusion should then catch up with masked diffusion or overtake it. We test this on the same models, trained only on full puzzles, by removing more and more hints and counting the samples that are both valid and distinct.

Without self conditioning, the prediction holds. Masked diffusion's lead on the full puzzles disappears as hints are removed, uniform diffusion finds more distinct valid solutions, and each hybrid follows its discrete view. The continuous models without self conditioning do not fit: they fail as hints are removed, which the Gaussian channel alone does not explain. With self conditioning, almost every model fills an empty board, but in the middle of the ladder its valid samples repeat more, as the fed-back prediction narrows the sampler onto fewer solutions. Switch the figure to "Compare samplers" to see each model's samplers.

### Self Conditioning Adds Graded Reliability

If self conditioning adds graded reliability, its effect should depend on how much reliability the corruption already gives and on the structure of the task.

#### Self Conditioning Helps the Mismatched Family

<div className="spark">

- On Sudoku, self conditioning lifts uniform diffusion and slightly lowers masked diffusion.
- On text8, it lifts masked diffusion and leaves uniform diffusion roughly unchanged.

</div>

Self conditioning should help most where a family is the bad match for the task ([the predictions](#how-we-study-the-axes)), and it does. On Sudoku, where the symmetric channel is the bad match, uniform diffusion gains a large amount while masked diffusion loses a little. On text8, where erasure is the bad match, masked diffusion gains while uniform diffusion barely moves, because the symmetric channel was already the best match. The figure above shows both tasks.

The same holds without tuning. Uniform diffusion's untuned sampler, which reveals tokens without any special order or correction, reaches its tuned score on Sudoku once self conditioning is on: graded reliability takes the place of the special sampler. Masked diffusion's untuned sampler also improves, but it stays well below the entropy-bounded sampler, which already gets most of the value of exact reliability through the order in which it reveals positions.

#### Why Masked Loses on Sudoku with Self Conditioning

<div className="spark">

- The drop comes from masked diffusion's entropy-bounded samplers, which reveal the surest positions first and rely on exact reliability.
- With its other samplers, self conditioning helps masked diffusion.

</div>

Self conditioning does not make masked diffusion worse as a model. It weakens the samplers that rely most on masked diffusion's exact reliability. An entropy-bounded sampler reveals positions in order of the model's confidence [@benhamu2025accelerated]. On an erasure channel this works like a peeling decoder: fill the positions that are already pinned, then repeat. Peeling needs exact knowledge of which positions are known, which masked diffusion provides. The fed-back prediction adds graded beliefs about the masked positions, which can blur the order the sampler relies on.

The figure breaks masked diffusion's Sudoku results down by sampler. The entropy-bounded samplers are its strongest with or without self conditioning, and they are the ones self conditioning lowers, while most other samplers gain, the untuned one included. We cannot run the same check on text8 or TinyStories, where the entropy-bounded samplers collapse even at their best settings.

By its response to self conditioning, uniform diffusion groups with the continuous methods, not with masked diffusion. None of these recipes tells the model exactly which positions to trust. #### What Does Not Fit: Simplex Diffusion on TinyStories

<div className="spark">

- Small-concentration simplex should behave like uniform, yet self conditioning hurts it on TinyStories.
- Its stories lose far more diversity than any other recipe's.
- This fits an over-sharpened sampler with a large vocabulary; a larger Dirichlet concentration should help, but is untested.

Small-concentration simplex diffusion should behave like uniform diffusion, yet on TinyStories self conditioning lowers its score while it raises uniform diffusion's. To separate the model from the effect of tuning, the figure compares samples from each model's untuned sampler. This fits an over-sharpened sampler. A small-concentration simplex state already looks like a committed token, the fed-back prediction adds confidence on top, and the sampler settles positions early, which MAUVE penalizes. ### Crossing the Commitment Axis

Hybrids and anchoring move recipes along the second axis, between committing hard tokens and refining soft values.

#### Hybrids Carry Graded Evidence, but the Schedule Fixes It

<div className="spark">

- Without self conditioning, many hybrids beat both of their parts; with it, the pure models catch up.
- Inside a hybrid, the learned schedule sends the continuous view to near pure noise early, for masked and uniform hybrids alike.

</div>

Hybrids capture part of what self conditioning provides, but not all of it. A hybrid's continuous view gives graded evidence, but it comes from the noise, and the schedule sets how much there is. Self conditioning's message is the model's own belief, which sharpens as sampling goes on.

Each row of the figure places a hybrid next to the two pure models it is built from. Without self conditioning, many hybrids on Sudoku and text8 beat both of their parts, though none does on TinyStories. With self conditioning, the pure models improve more than the hybrids, which at best tie their better part. A hybrid's own response to self conditioning follows its discrete view: uniform hybrids gain on Sudoku and lose on text8, while masked hybrids gain a little on text8.

#### Self Conditioning Lets Continuous Models Commit Tokens

<div className="spark">

- Continuous models never see committed tokens in training, so committing tokens usually hurts them.
- With self conditioning, random anchoring comes close to their best tuned results on Sudoku and improves on text8 and TinyStories.

Self conditioning weakens the commitment axis: with it, continuous models can commit tokens during sampling with much less damage. A continuous model never sees committed tokens in training, so a hard commitment pushes it away from what it knows. With the fed-back belief carried next to the state, the committed token is no longer the only thing the model sees about that position.

#### Renoising Collapses on text8 When the Two Signals Disagree

<div className="spark">

- With self conditioning, renoising collapses on text8 for recipes whose states hold hard tokens, but improves on Sudoku.
- On a synergistic task with many valid answers, the re-decoded tokens can disagree with the fed-back belief.

</div>

With self conditioning, renoising samplers collapse on text8 for every recipe whose state holds hard tokens, while they improve on Sudoku. A renoising sampler discards the current state, re-corrupts the predicted tokens and decodes again, while a self-conditioned model also receives its previous prediction. On a redundant task with a fixed answer, re-decoding a re-noised position returns the same token, so the new state and the fed-back belief agree. On a synergistic task with many valid answers, the re-decoded positions can settle on a different valid combination than the one the fed-back belief favors, and the two signals pull in different directions.

The figure breaks the masked and uniform text8 results down by sampler. Self conditioning improves most samplers, including native, the entropy-bounded samplers and both Ψ variants, while both renoising samplers collapse. Across recipes, renoising improves with self conditioning on Sudoku and collapses on text8 for every recipe whose state holds hard tokens, while pure Gaussian diffusion, whose state stays soft, holds up. TinyStories falls in between. This fits the explanation but is not a controlled test.

### Other Effects of Self Conditioning

<div className="spark">

- With self conditioning, special samplers matter less on prompted tasks.
- Sampling trajectories become more alike across recipes.

</div>

Two more results concern self conditioning itself rather than the reliability axis. Both fit the idea that the fed-back belief supplies what each state lacks.

<ExpandableSection title="With self conditioning, special samplers matter less on prompted tasks" client:load>

Without self conditioning, the best sampler for each recipe makes up for reliability its state lacks. On Sudoku, masked diffusion's best sampler is entropy-bounded and reveals positions in order of confidence. Uniform diffusion's best sampler is renoising, which re-corrupts the predicted tokens and decodes them again, a way to find wrong tokens without reliability information. The continuous methods mostly prefer Ψ-samplers [@deschenaux2026psi]. Once self conditioning supplies graded reliability, these samplers have less to make up for.

### Open Questions

<div className="spark">

- The task labels (Sudoku redundant, text8 synergistic) are argued from the tasks, not measured.
- Synthetic tasks with known copy or parity structure would test the claims with the labels fixed in advance.
- One seed and three small tasks; recent theory points to a second mechanism, the critical window.

</div>

The task labels rest on an argument from each task's structure, not on a measurement, and the readings above fit the results after the fact, on one training seed and three small tasks. A direct test would fix the structure in advance: synthetic tasks built from pure copies, or from parity checks of a chosen size, trained with masked, uniform and Gaussian corruption, with and without self conditioning. Redundant tasks should favor masked diffusion and synergistic ones uniform diffusion, with the gap growing as the parity checks span more positions. A second test would move one recipe along the reliability axis with everything else fixed: give uniform diffusion a flag that marks corrupted positions, or raise the concentration of simplex diffusion. Finally, recent theory traces the gap between masked diffusion and the other families in parallel sampling to a different mechanism: masked diffusion has a sharper critical window [@chen2026parallelism].

## Conclusion

<div className="spark">

- Different tasks need different corruptions: masking suits redundant structure, uniform noise suits synergistic structure, and Gaussian noise sits between them.
- Self conditioning helps each recipe according to what its corruption was missing, and moves every recipe toward the middle: a channel with graded reliability.
- To pick a recipe, ask: does the task have one answer or many, does the state say which positions to trust, and does the sampler commit before the model is sure?

</div>

We ablated self conditioning across masked, uniform, continuous and hybrid recipes, tuned the sampler for every model, and read the results through coding theory. Two conclusions follow.

First, different tasks need different corruptions. Coding theory predicts which corruption suits which task. Erasure, which is masking, suits redundant structure, where several clues pin each answer. The symmetric channel, which is uniform noise, suits synergistic structure, where the answer lies in combinations. Gaussian noise sits between them. Our results follow this: masked diffusion wins on prompted Sudoku, and uniform diffusion wins on text8.

Second, self conditioning does not help every recipe equally. It helps according to what the corruption was missing. It adds graded reliability, so it helps uniform diffusion most on Sudoku, where the symmetric channel is the bad match, helps masked diffusion on text8, where erasure is, and helps the continuous methods everywhere. In effect it moves every recipe toward the middle: a channel with graded reliability.

In practice, three questions help pick a recipe. Does the task have one answer or many? Does the noisy state tell the model which positions to trust? And does the sampler commit tokens before the model is sure?

## Related Work

<div className="spark">

- Critical windows: masked diffusion decides each position in a much narrower window than uniform or Gaussian diffusion, which costs it parallelism.
- SCUD: masking works because it builds in when each token is corrupted.
- Gourevitch et al.: uniform diffusion can be rewritten to sample like masked diffusion, and then it matches masked diffusion.

</div>

**Critical windows.** Li and Chen [@li2024critical] show that diffusion models decide each feature of the output in a short window of the reverse process. Chen and Wang [@chen2026parallelism] apply this to language models: masked diffusion's window is far narrower than uniform or Gaussian diffusion's, so it can need many more forward passes. Like ours, their split puts masked diffusion on one side and uniform and Gaussian diffusion on the other.

**Masked vs uniform diffusion.** Amin et al. [@amin2025scud] argue that masked diffusion works because it builds in when each token is corrupted and only learns what it becomes; giving other discrete processes the same knowledge (SCUD) recovers its benefits. Gourevitch et al. [@gourevitch2026uniform] rewrite uniform diffusion as an absorbing process with the same joint law, which turns its sampling into masked-diffusion-like steps: unmasking, with tokens carried over, plus a natural form of remasking. In that form it matches or beats masked diffusion, which suggests the gap between the two comes from sampling and parameterization more than from the noise itself. In our terms, both papers move uniform diffusion toward the erasure channel.

## Limitations, and the Future 

<div className="spark">

- The study trades depth for breadth; the limits and a path to larger studies follow.

</div>

### Limitations 

<div className="spark">

- Small scale: 8-layer models, 20,000 steps, small tasks.
- One training seed per model, and the same training settings for every recipe.
- Task-specific metrics, and the CDCD schedule for all continuous and hybrid models.

</div>

This study trades depth for breadth, and its conclusions carry the limits of that choice:
1. **Scale.** Every model is an 8-layer transformer trained for 20,000 steps on small tasks. Effects of self conditioning may change with model size, data and training length.
2. **One training seed.** Each recipe is trained once. We report the noise across sampling seeds and the bootstrap intervals of the tuning, but not the variation from retraining.
3. **Shared training settings.** All recipes use the same architecture, optimizer and learning rate. Some recipes may be disadvantaged by settings that suit others.
4. **Task metrics.** Sudoku accuracy, text8 valid words and TinyStories MAUVE each capture one aspect of quality.
5. **Schedules.** All continuous and hybrid models use the CDCD adaptive schedule. The fixed linear schedule is tested only for the hybrids. For discrete, we use uniform time sampling. 

### Towards a Method Factory 

<div className="spark">

- A full LLM pipeline adds more stages and more knobs than this study.
- The goal is a method factory: every component configurable, from pretraining and finetuning to RL and inference.
- Agents can keep runs healthy, tune samplers and spot inconsistencies, since every run is described by a config.
- Open questions: comparing likelihoods across families, scaling curves, choosing checkpoints, and what this post's protocol stands in for.

</div>

A full LLM pipeline only adds knobs: pretraining, supervised finetuning and reinforcement learning each bring their own choices on top of the ones studied here. The pipeline here is inspired by the Model Factory series from Poolside [@rowell2025modelfactory], where an experiment is described as a configuration and the system handles the rest. For DLMs we would want a *method factory*: plug-and-play components across the whole stack, from pretraining and architecture to RL and sampler inference. Agents would handle the small tasks, such as restarting failed runs, tuning samplers and running grid searches. The modular design makes this easy to hand off, since every run is described by a config and every number traces back to a saved one.

<MethodFactory client:visible />

A method factory would have to answer four open questions:

- Comparing methods whose likelihoods are not comparable (different bounds, continuous vs discrete), and whose RL dynamics may differ.
- Comparing methods that may scale differently with data and parameters: single points vs scaling curves.
- Choosing which pretraining checkpoint goes to SFT, and which SFT checkpoint goes to RL: likelihood, few-shot accuracy, or pass@k?
- Protocols in this post that stand in for scale: per-task sampler tuning on validation (instead of one inference setting macro-averaged across benchmarks), task-specific metrics (instead of benchmarks), one training seed.
