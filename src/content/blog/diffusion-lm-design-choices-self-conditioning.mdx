We ablate self conditioning across masked, uniform, continuous and hybrid recipes, *tuning the sampler for every model*. We read the results through **input reliability**: masked diffusion is an erasure channel, uniform diffusion a symmetric channel, and Gaussian diffusion sits in between. *First*, **which family wins depends on the task's structure**: masked wins on Sudoku, where each answer is pinned by several overlapping clues, and uniform wins on text8, where the answer lies in combinations. *Second*, **self conditioning adds graded reliability**, helping most the family that is the bad match: uniform gains 16.8 points on Sudoku, masked gains on text8, and the continuous methods gain everywhere. ## Introduction

<div className="spark">

- This part uses Part 1's codebase to study one question: what self conditioning does across recipes.
- Self conditioning reduces the spread in performance across sampler families.
- It lets pure continuous models commit tokens with less damage.
- Hybrids capture part of its benefit, but with self conditioning the pure models catch up.
- The results read through reliability: masked is an erasure channel, uniform a symmetric channel, Gaussian in between; redundant tasks favor masked, synergistic tasks favor uniform, and self conditioning helps the mismatched family.

</div>

In [Part 1](/blog/diffusion-lm-design-choices/) of the blog series, we introduced a modular codebase that covers a wide range of DLM recipes. In this part, we show how we can use that range to tease out answers for interesting questions, such as self conditioning. We first start by outlining the question, prior work within this direction, and why we think it is important. We then discuss what results we would ideally like to obtain, and what conclusions they could allow us to make. We dive into the experimental pipeline: the training setup, the choice of tasks, and the sampler tuning protocol. We then provide an overview of the results, which we summarize below: 
1. Self conditioning reduces the spread in performance across different sampler groups. 
2. Self conditioning makes pure continuous diffusion models amenable to discrete commitment, with performance in some cases close to the default untuned sampler for each recipe. 
3. Hybrid methods capture some of the benefits of self conditioning: without it, they often beat both pure discrete and pure continuous models, but some variants still gain from self conditioning, and with it the pure models catch up. 

The [results](#experimental-results) start with an explorer of every run. We then [read the results](#input-reliability-task-structure-and-self-conditioning) through coding theory. Masked diffusion is an erasure channel, uniform diffusion a symmetric channel, and Gaussian diffusion sits in between. Which channel is best depends on whether the task's structure is redundant, like Sudoku, or synergistic, like text8, and self conditioning adds graded reliability that helps most where a recipe is a bad match for the task. Eight findings follow, each with its own evidence.

Finally, we discuss the limitations of this experimental framework due to scale, and describe what a true "method factory" would enable. 

## The question: What does Self Conditioning Do? 

<div className="spark">

- Self conditioning began in Gaussian diffusion and is now standard there; it also helps simplex, spherical, uniform and masked diffusion.
- Existing explanations (pseudo-conditioning, a training and inference mismatch, fixed-point solvers) mostly target continuous diffusion.
- They do not explain why it helps discrete diffusion, for example in DiffusionGemma, so a general account is still open.

</div>

Self conditioning was originally introduced in Chen et al. [@chen2022analog] for a Gaussian diffusion method, where it demonstrated significant performance improvements. It was then adapted into embedding diffusion [@dieleman2022continuous] [@strudel2022self] [@gulrajani2023likelihood], and can be considered a standard part of Gaussian diffusion models [@huELFEmbeddedLanguage2026] [@chen2026langflow] [@yang2026continuousdiffusionscalescompetitively] [@chavan2026lafa] [@yang2026sigma] [@shabalin2025tencdm] [@meshchaninov2026cosmoscompressedsmoothlatent] [@meshchaninov2026train]. It also provides benefits for non-Gaussian methods, such as simplex diffusion, spherical interpolation, uniform diffusion, and masked diffusion [@deschenaux2026simplex] [@chemseddine2026spherical] [@joLoopholingDiscreteDiffusion2026]. Recently, Deschenaux et al. [@deschenaux2026loopholingwaylanguagemodeling] have demonstrated it can be used as a stand-alone generative method in its own right. 

While self conditioning is widely effective for DLM recipes, articulating what self conditioning does is still an active area of research. In [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], we hypothesized that it served as pseudo-conditioning input to address the long-tailed behavior of Gaussian noise in high dimensions. In [@davisDesignSpaceContinuous2026], they hypothesize that it serves to correct a training / inference mismatch. [@yooSelfconditionedFlowMap2026a] demonstrates that self conditioning in flow models can be related to fixed point solvers. 

Many of these explanations are restricted to why self conditioning is effective in continuous diffusion recipes. However, self conditioning is also effective in discrete settings -- DiffusionGemma [@diffusiongemma2026] uses self conditioning within a uniform diffusion model. [@joLoopholingDiscreteDiffusion2026] reports that loopholing, a very similar technique, also improves discrete diffusion performance. 

Most explanations fall short of explaining why it helps in these cases. If we take the explanation we proposed in [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion], then it is unclear why discrete anchoring would be beneficial for discrete diffusion methods. In [@davisDesignSpaceContinuous2026], while they devise a multi-layer diffusion with Gaussian, simplex, and discrete anchoring, they still find that self conditioning provides performance improvements, implying that there is still some benefit not fully explained. This leaves open a general theory of what self conditioning does, and how it impacts different DLM recipes spanning discrete, continuous, and hybrid methods. 

## Experimental Methodology

<div className="spark">

- This section covers the methods, the tasks, the training setup and the automated sampler tuning.

We then discuss the experimental protocol in detail, focusing especially on the automated sampler tuning protocol we use to ensure we get as close to the best performance for each checkpoint as possible. 

### Methods Studied 

<div className="spark">

- Continuous: Gaussian embedding diffusion, spherical interpolants (two variants) and simplex diffusion.
- Discrete: masked diffusion and uniform diffusion, with uniform in the posterior parameterization.
- Hybrids: one discrete view paired with one continuous view.
- Continuous and hybrid models use the CDCD adaptive schedule; discrete models use uniform time sampling.

</div>

We break down the general goal of understanding self conditioning into more digestible interaction studies -- we hold one family of methods fixed and ablate self conditioning to investigate performance differences. For continuous methods, we include the following corruption approaches: 
1. Gaussian embedding diffusion, essentially the recipe used in [@dieleman2022continuous]
2. Spherical interpolants, both the variant proposed in [@deschenaux2026language] and [@chemseddine2026spherical]
3. Simplex diffusion models as used in [@deschenaux2026simplex]

For discrete diffusion, we focus on masked diffusion [@sahooSimpleEffectiveMasked2024] and uniform diffusion. For uniform, we choose to work with a denoiser parameterization, as this allows us to fix the model parameterization across all methods [@gourevitch2026uniform]. 

For hybrid methods, we investigate the product space produced by the discrete methods and continuous methods. While our codebase does allow for combining different continuous views, we focus on discrete and continuous combinations as part of our goal is to understand how self conditioning affects performance if discrete anchoring is already provided to a continuous method. 

Continuous and hybrid models use the CDCD adaptive noise schedule [@dieleman2022continuous]; discrete models use uniform time sampling.

### Tasks 

<div className="spark">

- Sudoku: one correct answer per prompt, easy to score, but unlike real language.
- text8: open-ended characters, scored by distinct valid words of 5 or more letters.
- TinyStories: real language with a 10k BPE vocabulary, scored by MAUVE.
- Larger tasks such as OpenWebText or TinyGSM were not practical at this breadth.

We pick Sudoku, text8, and TinyStories as the tasks we apply each DLM recipe towards. Even at a small scale, these are not necessarily ideal -- the more informative battery of experiments would be to use pretraining on OpenWebText to understand convergence and generative quality; and TinyGSM as used in [@liu2023tinygsmachieving80gsm8k] [@deschenaux2026language]. However, given the sheer breadth of the design space we want to investigate for this study, it is simply not practical to use these tasks.

<TaskFrontier client:visible />

<ExpandableSection title="More detail: each task and its metric" client:load>

**Sudoku**

Sudoku is a classic reasoning task used in a variety of recent papers [@deschenaux2026language; @kim2025train; @agarwal2026posterior; @tang2026dbtm]. Additionally, it is the easiest to evaluate as we can directly read accuracy metrics. However, it is further from how actual language is structured for the following reasons: 
1. Given a prompt, the distribution is collapsed to a single point. Real text allows for multiple correct ways of responding to a prompt. 
2. The tokens are essentially uniform in terms of frequency. Real language is extremely asymmetric. 

For this reason, we need additional tasks to be able to properly gauge each method's performance under ablations.

**Text8**

We choose text8 as it represents a far less collapsed task: we can measure the number of unique and valid words with length greater than $n$ characters. For our experiments, we choose $5$. This is a useful task as it reflects whether methods are able to coordinate individual tokens together into longer spans of coherent text; and it also is relatively easy to measure. 

However, it is still quite distant from real language, as we work in the character space as opposed to the token space. As discussed in works such as [@pynadath2025candihybriddiscretecontinuousdiffusion] and [@lee2026onesteplanguagemodelingcontinuous], vocabulary size has a direct impact on the noise schedule, which could affect performance.

**TinyStories**

The most realistic task we look at is TinyStories, where we train a model to generate meaningful continuations of some story. This is the closest, as it uses a somewhat realistic vocabulary size (10k BPE) and is actual language generation. However, this also makes it the hardest to evaluate. It would not be appropriate to use generative perplexity and entropy, as these measure KL to the distribution encoded by the AR model. Furthermore, we would ideally like to take advantage of the ground truth continuations we have. We can choose SOL from [@sol2026], but this would pose significant difficulties for tuning, as our tuning stage of the pipeline is quite involved. BLEU would reward repeating generic phrases, which could result in overly generic stories. We choose to proceed with MAUVE from [@pillutla2021mauvemeasuringgapneural], using an AR model trained on TinyStories as the feature extractor. 

</ExpandableSection>

<div className="spark">

- Every recipe uses the same model and training: an 8-block DiT of width 512, 20,000 steps, batch 256, AdamW at 3e-4, EMA weights.
- Self conditioning: a no-gradient pass on 90% of each batch feeds back the expected embedding; at sampling time it adds no network calls.
- One training seed per model. Shared settings are a practical choice, not a principled one.

</div>

Every recipe uses the same model and training: an 8-block diffusion transformer of width 512, trained for 20,000 steps, with one training seed per model.

<ExpandableSection title="More detail: training settings" client:load>

- **Architecture.** A diffusion transformer (DiT) with 8 blocks, hidden size 512, 8 attention heads and dropout 0.1. Methods that diffuse in an embedding space (Gaussian, spherical, vMF) use 512-dimensional token embeddings.
- **Optimizer.** AdamW, learning rate 3e-4 with 2,500 linear warmup steps and no decay afterwards; betas (0.9, 0.999), no weight decay, gradient clipping at 1.0.
- **Length of training.** 20,000 optimizer steps at a global batch of 256 sequences (4 gradient-accumulation steps), bf16 mixed precision, on a single GPU.
- **EMA.** We keep an exponential moving average of the weights with decay 0.9999 and evaluate the EMA weights.
- **Sequence lengths.** Sudoku 180 tokens (puzzle prompt plus solution), text8 256 characters, TinyStories 512 BPE tokens (10k vocabulary).
- **Self conditioning.** On 90% of each training batch, the model first runs a no-gradient forward pass, and the expected embedding under that prediction is fed back as an extra input. At sampling time the previous step's prediction is fed back, so self conditioning adds no network calls.
- **Seeds.** One training seed per model. The noise this leaves is about 3 points on Sudoku, 0.5 words on text8 and 0.045 MAUVE on TinyStories.

The choice of using the same training settings for all methods is not based on principle, but practicality -- the ideal case would be to take each recipe and search for the best architectural choices, learning rate schedule, and optimizer as there is no guarantee that different generative methods have identical optimal settings. However, we choose not to do this as 1) these experiments are at such a small scale that the settings would not transfer to any practical scale; and 2) even without training sweeps, the number of runs and compute needed is large, for an academic scale.

### Sampler Pipeline

<div className="spark">

- Every model is tuned over the same eight samplers at 128 network calls; pure continuous models also get two random-anchoring samplers.
- Optuna searches on validation, each sampler's top 3 are re-scored on fresh prompts, and only the winner is tested once.
- Commit: freeze a position to one token. EB: commit the surest positions. Random anchoring: commit at random on a schedule. Ψ: mix native and renoise steps per position.
- Selection uses the task metric: Sudoku accuracy, text8 valid words, TinyStories MAUVE.
- One set of samplers runs on every recipe whose model predicts a clean-token posterior and whose process has both a forward and a reverse step (see the breakout).

</div>

We assess recipes by the final samples produced, making our results heavily contingent on the sampler performance for each method. To make sure we are comparing methods at their best performance, we rely heavily on automated sampler tuning for each recipe. We outline the sampler tuning pipeline below.

<TuningProcess client:visible />

<ExpandableSection title="More detail: the tuning protocol, step by step" client:load>

1. **Fixed budget.** Every sampler is run at 128 network calls (NFE), counting every call, including the final decoding call.
2. **Samplers.** Each model is tuned with eight samplers: native, native greedy, renoise, renoise greedy, EB greedy, EB churn, and Ψ on two clocks. The pure continuous models also get two random-anchoring samplers (anchor native, anchor renoise). The breakout below explains what each one changes, and how one set of samplers runs on every recipe.
3. **Search spaces.** Each sampler exposes a small set of knobs. All of them tune the prediction temperature (0.5 to 1.5). EB samplers also tune the entropy bound (0.02 to 0.5 nats). EB churn also tunes how often committed positions are re-noised and when that stops. Ψ tunes its schedule, step size and top-p. Anchoring tunes the commitment power (0.33 to 3) and whether committed tokens are sampled or taken greedily. The two "greedy" variants have only two settings, so both are simply evaluated.
4. **Search.** Optuna proposes settings: Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization for native, renoise and the EB samplers, and multivariate TPE for Ψ and anchoring, which have conditional or categorical knobs. Each search starts from the sampler's default settings and runs 2 to 24 trials depending on the size of the space.
5. **Stage 1 (screening).** Every trial is scored on 64 validation prompts with two sampling seeds.
6. **Stage 2 (confirmation).** Each sampler's top 3 settings are re-scored on 256 fresh validation prompts with a fresh seed, with a bootstrap interval. The best stage-2 result across all samplers is the model's tuned sampler. Re-scoring on fresh prompts guards against picking a setting that only got lucky on the stage-1 prompts.
7. **Test.** Only the winner is scored on the test split, once, at 128 NFE with two seeds. Untuned results use each model's native sampler at temperature 1 with the same budget.

<AnchorSchedule client:visible />

</ExpandableSection>

Three terms are used throughout the results. A sampler **commits** a position when it sets that position to one token and freezes it for the rest of sampling. **Entropy-bounded (EB)** samplers commit the positions the model is surest about [@benhamu2025accelerated]: at each step they take positions from most to least certain, for as long as the summed entropy of the chosen positions, not counting the largest one, stays under a tuned bound. **Random anchoring** ignores the model's confidence and commits positions at random on a schedule, which mimics the order a masked process imposes. Finally, **Ψ** mixes the native and renoise samplers [@deschenaux2026psi]: at each step, every position takes the native step with some probability and is re-noised from the model's prediction otherwise.

The metric that drives selection is the task metric: exact-solve accuracy for Sudoku, distinct valid words of five or more letters for text8, and MAUVE for TinyStories. The full sampler-tuning protocol is summarized in [Figure T](#sampler-pipeline).

### What a sampler is allowed to ask for

A recipe in our codebase is built from **views**, one per corruption; a hybrid has two. Each view bundles a representation (tokens to states), a schedule (time to noise coefficients) and a process (the corruption and its reverse). The network makes a single prediction that all views share. A sampler touches a recipe only through this short list:

| The sampler needs | Supplied by |
|---|---|
| A clean-token posterior $p_\theta(x_0 \mid z_t)$ at every position | the model, in the posterior parameterization |
| A starting state at $t = 1$ | the process's prior |
| The native reverse step $z_t \to z_s$, given that posterior | the process |
| The forward kernel $q(z_s \mid x_0)$, to re-noise a clean guess | the process |
| The clean state of a token, to commit a position | the representation |
| Final tokens | one terminal prediction, decoded greedily or by sampling |

So **any recipe whose model predicts a clean-token posterior, and whose process can write both its forward kernel and its native reverse step, can run every sampler below.** That covers masked, uniform, Gaussian, spherical, simplex and the hybrids. The exceptions are recipes whose network does not output a posterior: SEDD predicts score ratios and keeps its own analytic sampler. A plug-in estimate, as in DUO, can be converted to a posterior; we train uniform diffusion with the posterior parameterization directly, so every model in this post predicts the same kind of object. The codebase checks each pairing before sampling and refuses an incompatible one with a reason, and a generated table records every family-by-sampler combination.

### One loop with five choices

Every sampler below runs the same loop. Each step makes one network call, then fills five slots:

1. **Clean estimate.** Keep the full posterior, sample one token per position from it, or take its most likely token.
2. **Positions.** Update all positions, or choose some: at random, by entropy, or on a fixed schedule.
3. **Transition.** Move the state with the process's native reverse step, or discard it and re-noise the clean estimate with the forward kernel.
4. **Commitment.** None, freeze (a committed position is set to its token's clean state and stays there), or revise (committed positions can be released again).
5. **Terminal decoding.** Greedy or sampled, chosen separately from the slots above.

Every run uses 127 updates plus one terminal call, so 128 network calls in all. Temperature and top-p belong to the model's prediction, not to the sampler, so every sampler can tune them.

### The sampler classes

**Native (and native greedy).** Each process's own reverse step, at every position. In masked diffusion, a masked position is revealed with probability $(\alpha_s - \alpha_t)/(1 - \alpha_t)$ and its token is drawn from the posterior; revealed positions stay. In uniform diffusion, the step mixes the exact bridges $q(z_s \mid z_t, x_0)$ over the posterior. In Gaussian diffusion, it is a stochastic step from the reverse posterior around the expected clean embedding. Simplex and spherical diffusion use their own bridge and tangent-flow steps. *Native greedy* feeds the reverse step a one-hot argmax instead of the full posterior. Slots: posterior, all positions, native step, no commitment.

**Renoise (and renoise greedy).** Sample a clean sequence $\hat x_0$ from the posterior, then draw the next state fresh from the forward kernel, $z_s \sim q(z_s \mid \hat x_0)$. The current state is discarded. This needs only a posterior and a forward kernel, so it runs on every process, and it can re-mask a revealed token or move a settled point. *Renoise greedy* takes $\hat x_0$ as the argmax. Slots: sampled (or argmax) tokens, all positions, forward kernel, no commitment.

**Ψ.** A mixture of the two above, drawn per position [@deschenaux2026psi]: each position takes the native step with probability $\kappa_t$ and is re-noised otherwise. $\kappa_t = 1$ is native and $\kappa_t = 0$ is renoise, token for token. The schedule for $\kappa_t$ comes from ReMDM's remasking scale [@wang2025remasking]. Ψ was introduced for discrete diffusion; we extend it to continuous processes by re-noising with each process's own forward kernel. Tuned: the schedule shape, its strength, top-p and temperature.

**Entropy-bounded: EB greedy and EB churn.** At each step, every uncommitted position is scored by the entropy of its predicted distribution. Positions are taken from most to least certain while the summed entropy, not counting the largest, stays under a tuned bound $\gamma$ [@benhamu2025accelerated]. Each chosen position is committed to its most likely token and frozen; the rest take the native step. In a masked model, committing is revealing. In a continuous model, committing sets the position's state to its token's clean embedding and holds it there, so even a Gaussian model can decode in order of confidence. *EB churn* also releases a few committed positions at random each step and re-noises them with the forward kernel, at a tuned rate that drops to zero partway through sampling. Slots: argmax tokens, entropy-chosen positions, native step for the rest, freeze (churn: revise).

**Random anchoring (continuous models only).** Commitment without the model's confidence. After step $k$ of $S$, a share $(k/S)^p$ of positions is committed, chosen uniformly at random; the last step commits the rest. Each new position gets a token sampled from (or the argmax of) the prediction at that step. The other positions take the native step (*anchor native*) or are re-noised (*anchor renoise*). This mimics the reveal schedule of a masked process on a continuous model. Slots: sampled or argmax tokens, scheduled random positions, native step or forward kernel, freeze.

### What this does and does not claim

Running every sampler on every recipe makes the comparisons fair: each recipe gets the same chances to find a good sampler. It does not make every pairing principled. Renoising a masked model re-masks tokens that the masked reverse process would never re-mask, and entropy-bounded commitment freezes positions that a Gaussian process never freezes. These are valid ways to produce a sample, not the exact reverse of the process the model was trained on. That is why we tune and select on validation rather than assume one sampler is right for each recipe.

Code: `core/samplers/composed.py` (the shared loop), `core/samplers/policies.py` (the slots), `core/samplers/psi.py`, `core/samplers/churn.py`, and the generated compatibility table in `docs/validation/compatibility.md`.

</div>

</details>

## Input Reliability, Task Structure, and Self Conditioning

<div className="spark">

- Claim 1: input reliability, how much the noisy state says about which positions to trust, is a main axis for comparing recipes.
- Masked diffusion is an erasure channel, uniform diffusion a symmetric channel, and Gaussian diffusion sits in between.
- Which channel is best depends on the task: erasure suits redundant structure (Sudoku), the symmetric channel suits synergistic structure (text8).
- Claim 2: self conditioning adds graded reliability to every recipe, and helps most where the recipe is a bad match for the task.

</div>

Based on the results, we claim the following. First, **input reliability is a main axis for comparing recipes**: how much the noisy state tells the model about whether each position can be trusted. Second, **self conditioning adds graded reliability to every recipe**, and what that does depends on how much reliability the recipe already gives and on the structure of the task.

### Three Kinds of Corruption

<div className="spark">

- Masked: an erasure channel. Exact reliability: a position is revealed and correct, or masked and empty.
- Uniform: a symmetric channel. The same reliability everywhere: a swapped token looks like any other.
- Gaussian: in between. Each position gets its own graded reliability.

</div>

Under the usual split, masked and uniform diffusion are siblings, and the continuous methods sit on the other side. Coding theory gives each family a different channel instead.

- **Masked diffusion is an erasure channel.** A position is either revealed and correct, or masked and empty, and the model can see which. Reliability is exact.
- **Uniform diffusion is a symmetric channel.** A corrupted token is replaced by another valid token, so every position looks the same whether or not it was swapped. Reliability is the same everywhere, and only context can find the swapped tokens. The binary version is the binary symmetric channel.
- **Gaussian diffusion sits in between.** Noise is added to each position's embedding, and each position gets its own graded reliability, set by how much closer the noisy point is to the nearest token than to the runner-up.

Simplex diffusion slides between the last two: at a small Dirichlet concentration it reduces to uniform diffusion, and at a large one it follows a path with Gaussian fluctuations [@deschenaux2026simplex]. Our simplex models use a small concentration, so they sit near the uniform end. Whether the state commits hard tokens or carries soft values is a second axis ([Figure L](#figure-l)).

<div id="figure-l"></div>

<ReliabilityMap client:visible kicker="Figure L · two axes" />

<ExpandableSection title="Reliability as a log-likelihood ratio" client:load>

For one position, let $L$ be the log-likelihood ratio between two candidate clean tokens given the noisy state. Its size $|L|$ is the reliability.

- **Erasure.** A revealed position has $|L| = \infty$ and a masked one has $L = 0$: two spikes, and the model sees which.
- **Symmetric.** If a token is replaced with probability $\beta$ by one drawn uniformly from a vocabulary of size $V$, then for an observed token $y$ the ratio between "the clean token is $y$" and "it is some other $z$" is $\log \frac{1 - \beta + \beta / V}{\beta / V}$, the same at every position: one spike. For a binary symmetric channel with flip probability $p$, $|L| = \log\frac{1-p}{p}$ everywhere.
- **Gaussian.** For one bit sent as $\pm 1$ with noise level $\sigma$, $L = 2Y/\sigma^2$, which given $X = +1$ is normal with mean $2/\sigma^2$ and variance $4/\sigma^2$ [@richardson2008modern] (§4.1.5): a whole spread of reliabilities. For tokens, each comparison against a competitor behaves like this one-bit case, so the spread is widest against close competitors.

</ExpandableSection>

### Which Channel Suits Which Task: Redundant vs Synergistic Structure

<div className="spark">

- Redundant (copy-like): several clues each pin the same answer. Synergistic (parity-like): the answer lies only in a combination.
- At equal capacity, erasure is best and the symmetric channel worst for redundant structure; for synergistic structure, the order flips. Gaussian is always in between. 
- Prompted Sudoku is redundant: the givens are never corrupted, the answer is fixed, and each revealed cell is a clue to the same solution that helps on its own.
- text8 is synergistic: no single character is wrong by itself; a word is valid only as a whole, and many words are possible.

</div>

Tasks differ in how the answer depends on the positions around it. In **redundant** (copy-like) structure, several clues each pin the same answer, so any one of them is enough. In **synergistic** (parity-like) structure, the answer lies only in a combination: no single clue says much, and the clues together say everything. These are the two poles of O-information [@rosas2019quantifying]; a copy is the purest redundancy and a parity check the purest synergy.

Coding theory tells us which channel suits each. At equal capacity, the erasure channel gives the most information about a value seen several times and the symmetric channel the least; for a parity of several values, the order flips, and the Gaussian channel always sits in between. This is the result known as the extremes of information combining [@richardson2008modern] (§4.10.2). The intuition is that erasure is all or nothing. For a copy, one revealed look is enough. For a parity, every other position must be revealed, which rarely happens, while a symmetric channel leaves every position slightly unsure but the combination still informative.

**Our two main tasks sit at opposite poles.**

- **Sudoku, in its prompted form, is redundant.** The prompt determines the answer, and each answer cell is pinned by its row, its column and its box at once: three overlapping clues for one value.
- **text8 is synergistic.** No single character is wrong by itself. A word is valid only if all its letters make sense together, and many different words are possible. The metric rewards exactly this: it counts valid words of five or more letters, an all-or-nothing check over a group. Text also has some redundant pieces ("q" is almost always followed by "u"), but the structure the task rewards is mainly synergistic.
- **TinyStories** is a prompted task with many valid continuations, so it falls in between.

<ExpandableSection title="More detail: why prompted Sudoku is redundant" client:load>

Sudoku's rules look synergistic: in a row, no single digit says much about another, and only the nine together must be all different. What matters for the channel view, though, is the evidence that passes *through the channel*, and in our task the prompt shapes it.

- **The givens are never corrupted.** Only the answer cells are noised. The rules tie the answer cells to the givens as much as to each other, and the givens always arrive clean, so the joint structure is already settled by the prompt.
- **Given the prompt, the answer is fixed.** Every answer cell is a function of the same prompt, so every answer cell the channel reveals points to the same single solution. A revealed cell never argues for a second valid answer.
- **Each revealed cell helps on its own.** Take one blank cell. The givens in its row, column and box have already ruled out most digits. Each other answer cell in those units that the channel reveals can rule out one more, whether or not its neighbors are revealed, and the cell can often be pinned by its row, its column or its box separately. The evidence adds up piece by piece, from several directions at once. In a pure parity check, by contrast, knowing all but one of the other positions tells you nothing; the answer appears only when the last one arrives.

That is the copy-like signature: several partial clues about one fixed value, each useful alone. It also shows why the symmetric channel does badly here. An erased neighbor says nothing, which is harmless. A swapped neighbor shows a wrong digit, and if that digit happens to be the blank cell's true value, it rules out the right answer. The erasure channel never lies, so every clue it passes can be trusted as it arrives.

**This depends on the prompt.** A model that generates complete grids from scratch faces the rules alone: no answer is fixed, and the all-different constraints tie the generated digits together synergistically. The channel view then predicts the opposite ordering, with uniform diffusion closing the gap to masked diffusion or overtaking it. We have not run that variant.

</ExpandableSection>

### What Self Conditioning Adds

<div className="spark">

- Self conditioning feeds back the model's belief about every position: graded reliability that follows the model, not the noise.
- It should help most the family that is the bad match for the task, and least the family that is already the good match.
- On Gaussian it makes the existing graded reliability more flexible, so it should help on both tasks.

</div>

Self conditioning feeds the model's previous prediction back as an input. That prediction says how sure the model was about every position, so it adds graded reliability on top of whatever the state already gives. Combined with the task structure, this predicts:

| | Sudoku (redundant) | text8 (synergistic) |
|---|---|---|
| Which family wins without SC | Masked: erasure is best for redundancy | Uniform: the symmetric channel is best for synergy |
| SC on masked | Little to gain; graded beliefs can blur its exact reliability | Room to gain: erasure is the worst channel here |
| SC on uniform | Room to gain: the symmetric channel is the worst here | Little to gain: already the best channel |
| SC on Gaussian | Gains: its margins now follow the model's belief, not only the noise | Gains, for the same reason |

**Hybrids carry graded evidence too, but of a fixed kind.** A hybrid's continuous view adds graded evidence to the state, but it comes from the noise, and the schedule sets how much there is. Self conditioning passes the model's own belief, which sharpens as sampling goes on, and it adds this to any recipe without changing its corruption.

#### Why Masked Loses on Sudoku with Self Conditioning

<div className="spark">

- Masked diffusion's drop on Sudoku comes from its entropy-bounded samplers, its best samplers.
- These reveal the surest positions first, like a peeling decoder, and rely on exact reliability; self conditioning blurs it.
- With the untuned native sampler, self conditioning helps masked diffusion on every task.

</div>

Self conditioning does not make masked diffusion worse as a model. It weakens the samplers that rely most on masked diffusion's exact reliability. An entropy-bounded sampler reveals positions in order of the model's confidence [@benhamu2025accelerated]. On an erasure channel this works like a peeling decoder: fill the positions that are already pinned, then repeat. Peeling needs exact knowledge of which positions are known, which masked diffusion provides. The fed-back prediction adds graded beliefs about the masked positions, which can blur the order the sampler relies on.

On Sudoku, the entropy-bounded samplers are masked diffusion's strongest samplers with or without self conditioning, and self conditioning lowers both. On validation, entropy-bounded greedy falls from 95.7% to 93.0% and entropy-bounded churn from 95.3% to 92.2%, and the selected sampler's test score falls from 97.4% to 93.8%. The other samplers gain: with the untuned native sampler, self conditioning raises masked diffusion on every task, from 31.7% to 81.6% on Sudoku. We cannot run the same check on text8 or TinyStories, where the entropy-bounded samplers collapse even at their best settings.

- Small-concentration simplex should behave like uniform, yet self conditioning hurts it on TinyStories.
- Its stories lose far more diversity: distinct bigrams fall from 0.248 to 0.130, against 0.253 for real stories.
- This fits an over-sharpened sampler with a 10k vocabulary; a larger Dirichlet concentration should help, but is untested.

</div>

Small-concentration simplex diffusion should behave like uniform diffusion, yet on TinyStories self conditioning hurts simplex (0.889 to 0.858 with the best tuned sampler) while it helps uniform.

To separate the base model from the effect of tuning, we compare samples from each model's untuned native sampler, 4,096 stories per model. Self conditioning lowers diversity for every recipe, but for most it lands near the diversity of real stories. Simplex loses far more and ends well below it: distinct bigrams across its stories fall from 0.248 to 0.130, against 0.253 for real stories, and the share of stories that open with the same ten words rises from 3.3% to 11.0%. The pure continuous methods also drop somewhat below real stories (distinct bigrams around 0.21), yet their MAUVE rises, so a loss of diversity alone does not explain the drop.

<SimplexDiversity client:visible kicker="Figure · simplex diversity" />

**A possible cause.** This fits an over-sharpened sampler. A small-concentration simplex state already looks like a committed token, the fed-back prediction adds confidence on top, and the sampler settles positions early, which MAUVE penalizes. It happens only on TinyStories because of its vocabulary: with 10,000 tokens, a near-corner simplex point looks far more like a committed token than it does with the 27 characters of text8 or the digits of Sudoku. Raising the Dirichlet concentration should move simplex toward the Gaussian end of [Figure L](#figure-l) (Proposition 4.1 of [@deschenaux2026simplex]), though we have not tested it. Simplex's stories without self conditioning are also unusually short (101 words, against about 130 for the other recipes and 132 for real stories), so something specific to this recipe may be at work as well.

### Open Questions

<div className="spark">

- The task labels (Sudoku redundant, text8 synergistic) are argued from the tasks, not measured.
- Synthetic tasks with known copy or parity structure would test the claims with the labels fixed in advance.
- One seed and three small tasks; recent theory points to a second mechanism, the critical window.

</div>

The task labels rest on an argument from each task's structure, not on a measurement, and the readings above fit the results after the fact, on one training seed and three small tasks. A direct test would fix the structure in advance: synthetic tasks built from pure copies, or from parity checks of a chosen size, trained with masked, uniform and Gaussian corruption, with and without self conditioning. Redundant tasks should favor masked diffusion and synergistic ones uniform diffusion, with the gap growing as the parity checks span more positions. A second test would move one recipe along the reliability axis with everything else fixed: give uniform diffusion a flag that marks corrupted positions, or raise the concentration of simplex diffusion. Finally, recent theory traces the gap between masked diffusion and the other families in parallel sampling to a different mechanism: masked diffusion has a sharper critical window [@chen2026parallelism].

## Related Work

<div className="spark">

- Critical windows: masked diffusion decides each position in a much narrower window than uniform or Gaussian diffusion, which costs it parallelism.
- SCUD: masking works because it builds in when each token is corrupted.
- Gourevitch et al.: uniform diffusion can be rewritten to sample like masked diffusion, and then it matches masked diffusion.

</div>

**Critical windows.** Li and Chen [@li2024critical] show that diffusion models decide each feature of the output in a short window of the reverse process. Chen and Wang [@chen2026parallelism] apply this to language models: masked diffusion's window is far narrower than uniform or Gaussian diffusion's, so it can need many more forward passes. Like ours, their split puts masked diffusion on one side and uniform and Gaussian diffusion on the other.

**Masked vs uniform diffusion.** Amin et al. [@amin2025scud] argue that masked diffusion works because it builds in when each token is corrupted and only learns what it becomes; giving other discrete processes the same knowledge (SCUD) recovers its benefits. Gourevitch et al. [@gourevitch2026uniform] rewrite uniform diffusion as an absorbing process with the same joint law, which turns its sampling into masked-diffusion-like steps: unmasking, with tokens carried over, plus a natural form of remasking. In that form it matches or beats masked diffusion, which suggests the gap between the two comes from sampling and parameterization more than from the noise itself. In our terms, both papers move uniform diffusion toward the erasure channel.

## Limitations, and the Future 

<div className="spark">

- The study trades depth for breadth; the limits and a path to larger studies follow.

</div>

### Limitations 

<div className="spark">

- Small scale: 8-layer models, 20,000 steps, small tasks.
- One training seed per model, and the same training settings for every recipe.
- Task-specific metrics, and the CDCD schedule for all continuous and hybrid models.

</div>

This study trades depth for breadth, and its conclusions carry the limits of that choice:
1. **Scale.** Every model is an 8-layer transformer trained for 20,000 steps on small tasks. Effects of self conditioning may change with model size, data and training length.
2. **One training seed.** Each recipe is trained once. We report the noise across sampling seeds and the bootstrap intervals of the tuning, but not the variation from retraining.
3. **Shared training settings.** All recipes use the same architecture, optimizer and learning rate. Some recipes may be disadvantaged by settings that suit others.
4. **Task metrics.** Sudoku accuracy, text8 valid words and TinyStories MAUVE each capture one aspect of quality.
5. **Schedules.** All continuous and hybrid models use the CDCD adaptive schedule. The fixed linear schedule is tested only for the hybrids. For discrete, we use uniform time sampling. 

### Towards a Method Factory 

<div className="spark">

- A full LLM pipeline adds more stages and more knobs than this study.
- The goal is a method factory: every component configurable, from pretraining and finetuning to RL and inference.
- Agents can keep runs healthy, tune samplers and spot inconsistencies, since every run is described by a config.
- Open questions: comparing likelihoods across families, scaling curves, choosing checkpoints, and what this post's protocol stands in for.

</div>

A full LLM pipeline only adds knobs: pretraining, supervised finetuning and reinforcement learning each bring their own choices on top of the ones studied here. The pipeline here is inspired by the Model Factory series from Poolside [@rowell2025modelfactory], where an experiment is described as a configuration and the system handles the rest. For DLMs we would want a *method factory*: plug-and-play components across the whole stack, from pretraining and architecture to RL and sampler inference. Agents would handle the small tasks, such as restarting failed runs, tuning samplers and running grid searches. The modular design makes this easy to hand off, since every run is described by a config and every number traces back to a saved one.

<MethodFactory client:visible />

A method factory would have to answer four open questions:

- Comparing methods whose likelihoods are not comparable (different bounds, continuous vs discrete), and whose RL dynamics may differ.
- Comparing methods that may scale differently with data and parameters: single points vs scaling curves.
- Choosing which pretraining checkpoint goes to SFT, and which SFT checkpoint goes to RL: likelihood, few-shot accuracy, or pass@k?
- Protocols in this post that stand in for scale: per-task sampler tuning on validation (instead of one inference setting macro-averaged across benchmarks), task-specific metrics (instead of benchmarks), one training seed.
